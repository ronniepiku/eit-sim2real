# EIT Touch Classification — Noise Model Specification

## Overview

This document details the physical justification, mathematical formulation, and
implementation of the 4-component noise model used in the EIT simulation pipeline.

The noise model is the primary methodological contribution of this project. It enables
systematic ablation studies to determine which noise sources most affect classifier
robustness during simulation-to-reality transfer.

## Design Principles

1. **Physics-grounded**: Each component models a distinct, documented physical degradation source
2. **Independently configurable**: Components can be toggled on/off for ablation studies
3. **Literature-parameterised**: All default values derive from published EIT hardware characterisations
4. **Per-electrode structure**: Contact impedance and bias are applied per-electrode (not per-measurement), reflecting that these are properties of the electrode–substrate interface
5. **Fixed application order**: Components are applied sequentially (Gaussian → Contact Impedance → Bias → Quantisation), reflecting the physical signal chain

## Component Specifications

### 1. Gaussian Measurement Noise

**Physical origin**: Every analogue measurement system is subject to stochastic
perturbations arising from multiple independent physical processes within the
signal acquisition chain. In EIT hardware, the dominant contributors are:

- **Johnson–Nyquist (thermal) noise**: Random voltage fluctuations generated by
  thermal agitation of charge carriers within the electrode material and
  interconnect traces. The spectral density is proportional to temperature and
  resistance: $S_v = 4k_BTR$ (Johnson, 1928; Nyquist, 1928).
- **Amplifier input-referred noise**: Operational amplifiers in the voltage
  measurement front-end contribute both voltage noise (typically 1–10 nV/√Hz for
  instrumentation amplifiers) and current noise that develops a voltage across
  source impedance (Horowitz & Hill, 2015, Ch. 8).
- **Electromagnetic interference (EMI)**: Capacitive and inductive coupling from
  mains wiring, digital switching transients, and RF sources inject common-mode
  and differential-mode noise. Although differential measurement rejects
  common-mode EMI, residual imbalance in electrode impedances converts a fraction
  to differential noise (Rosell et al., 1988).
- **Quantised current-source fluctuations**: Finite precision of the excitation
  current source produces small amplitude variations that propagate through the
  measurement as additive noise on the voltage readings.

These processes are mutually independent and, by the central limit theorem, their
aggregate effect on each voltage measurement is well-approximated by a Gaussian
distribution (Adler & Lionheart, 2006).

**Justification for inclusion in the noise model**: The EIDORS forward solver
computes voltages deterministically from the conductivity distribution and mesh
geometry, assuming ideal instrumentation with infinite precision. It does not
model any stochastic processes in the measurement electronics. Gaussian noise
must therefore be injected post-hoc to bridge the gap between the idealised
simulation output and the noisy signals observed in physical hardware. Without
this component, classifiers trained on simulation data would be exposed only to
noiseless signals and would lack robustness to the irreducible measurement
uncertainty present in all real EIT systems (Adler & Lionheart, 2006; Isaacson
et al., 2004).

**Mathematical formulation**:
```
Δv' = Δv + n
n ~ N(0, σ_n² I)
σ_n = (||Δv||₂ / ||n̂||₂) × 10^(-SNR/20)   when ||Δv|| > 0
σ_n = noise_floor × n_meas / ||n̂||₂          when ||Δv|| = 0
```

where `n̂ ~ N(0, I)` is a unit-variance noise realisation.

**Parameters**:
| Parameter | Default | Range | Source |
|-----------|---------|-------|--------|
| SNR (dB) | 40 | 30–80 | Adler & Lionheart (2006) |
| noise_floor (V RMS) | 1×10⁻⁴ | — | Typical EIT baseline noise |

**Notes**: The signal-dependent formulation ensures noise scales proportionally with
measurement amplitude, consistent with the observation that EIT systems maintain
approximately constant SNR across operating conditions (Holder, 2004, §2.4). A fixed
noise floor is applied for zero-signal measurements (e.g., the "no contact" class)
to ensure realistic baseline noise even when the differential signal is identically
zero. The 40 dB default represents the lower bound for wearable/portable hardware;
laboratory systems typically achieve 60–80 dB (Adler & Lionheart, 2006), while
textile-electrode wearable systems may operate at 30–40 dB (Russo et al., 2017).

### 2. Electrode Contact Impedance Variation

**Physical origin**: In electrical impedance tomography, current is injected and
voltage is measured through electrodes placed on a body surface. The contact
impedance is the impedance of the thin interface layer between each electrode and
the underlying tissue or substrate. It is not a property of the bulk medium being
imaged, but of the electrode–skin (or electrode–substrate) boundary itself. Contact
impedance variation arises from several distinct physical mechanisms:

- **Electrochemical double-layer effects**: At the metal–electrolyte interface, a
  charge double layer forms whose impedance depends on electrode material, surface
  roughness, and local ionic concentration. This is an intrinsic property of the
  electrode–tissue electrochemistry (Grimnes & Martinsen, 2015, Ch. 7).
- **Spatially non-uniform skin preparation**: In biomedical EIT, skin is typically
  abraded or conductive gel is applied to reduce impedance. Uneven gel application,
  variable stratum corneum thickness, and differing local hydration produce
  electrode-to-electrode impedance variation (Boone & Holder, 1996).
- **Mechanical contact quality**: Partial lift-off, variable pressure, or poor
  conformance to curved anatomy means the effective contact area differs between
  electrodes. A partially-detached electrode exhibits substantially higher contact
  impedance than one firmly pressed against the surface (Vilhunen et al., 2002).
- **Temporal drift**: Gel drying, sweat accumulation, and subject movement cause
  contact impedance to change over time, introducing non-stationary variation
  across the electrode array (Malmivuo & Plonsey, 1995, Ch. 25).
- **Manufacturing tolerances**: In wearable or textile-electrode systems,
  fabrication variability means each electrode's effective area and surface
  chemistry differ from the nominal specification.

In an ideal system with zero contact impedance, all injected current flows into
the body and voltages are measured directly on the body surface. In practice, a
voltage drop occurs across each electrode's contact impedance layer. Since this
drop varies from electrode to electrode, it introduces a multiplicative distortion:
measurements associated with higher-impedance electrodes are systematically scaled
relative to others.

**Justification for inclusion in the noise model**: The EIDORS Complete Electrode
Model (CEM) does incorporate contact impedance via the boundary condition:

$$U_\ell = u|_{e_\ell} + z_\ell \frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu}\bigg|_{e_\ell}$$

where $U_\ell$ is the measured voltage at electrode $\ell$, $u|_{e_\ell}$ is the
true boundary potential, $z_\ell$ is the contact impedance, and
$\partial u / \partial \nu$ is the current density normal to the electrode surface
(Cheng et al., 1989; Somersalo et al., 1992). However, in this simulation pipeline,
the forward model assigns a single, fixed, uniform contact impedance value to all
electrodes (the EIDORS default). The forward solve therefore produces voltages
assuming an ideal, symmetric configuration. In physical hardware, each electrode
has a different and unknown contact impedance. The noise model captures this
mismatch—the stochastic, per-electrode variation around the nominal value assumed
by the simulation—rather than duplicating the CEM physics. Re-solving the forward
model with perturbed per-electrode impedances for every sample would be
computationally prohibitive; for small perturbations (5–20%), the multiplicative
approximation closely matches a full CEM re-solve, as shown by the linearised
analysis of Vilhunen et al. (2002, §3.2). This post-hoc formulation also permits
ablation at experiment time without regenerating the dataset.

**Mathematical formulation**:
```
Δv'_i = Δv_i × z_e(i)
z_e = exp(ε_e)
ε_e ~ N(0, σ_z²)
```

where `e(i)` maps measurement `i` to the electrode responsible for that measurement
block. Each electrode has a single impedance factor applied to all measurements it
participates in.

**Parameters**:
| Parameter | Default | Range | Source |
|-----------|---------|-------|--------|
| σ_z (std as fraction) | 0.10 (10%) | 0.05–0.20 | Vilhunen et al. (2002) |
| n_electrodes | 16 | — | System configuration |

**Notes**: The log-normal model ensures $z > 0$ (impedance is strictly positive) and
produces asymmetric variation consistent with the physical constraint that contact
impedance cannot be negative. Applied per-electrode (not per-measurement) as contact
impedance is a property of the electrode–skin interface, not of individual voltage
readings. Vilhunen et al. (2002) explicitly demonstrate that unknown contact
impedance variation remains a dominant error source even when the CEM is used in
reconstruction, motivating joint reconstruction of impedances alongside internal
conductivity.

### 3. Electrode Positioning Bias

**Physical origin**: In EIT, the forward model and reconstruction algorithms assume
electrodes are located at precisely known positions on the body surface. In practice,
electrode placement deviates from the nominal geometry due to several sources of
systematic error:

- **Manual placement inaccuracy**: When electrodes are applied by hand (clinical or
  laboratory settings), positioning errors of 1–5 mm are typical, even with
  measurement guides. On a 16-electrode system with circumference ~300 mm, this
  corresponds to angular errors of 1–6° (Kolehmainen et al., 1997).
- **Manufacturing tolerances in electrode arrays**: Rigid or flexible printed
  electrode arrays (e.g., for wearable EIT) are subject to fabrication tolerances
  in trace routing and electrode pad placement, producing systematic offsets from
  the design geometry.
- **Anatomical variation between subjects**: Electrode belts or sleeves designed
  for a nominal limb circumference will sit at different angular positions when
  applied to subjects with different arm geometries. Electrodes may also shift
  along the limb axis on tapered forearms.
- **Electrode migration during use**: Adhesive degradation, subject movement, and
  skin elasticity cause electrodes to drift from their initial positions over the
  course of a measurement session.

When an electrode is displaced from its assumed position, the relationship between
the true boundary voltage at that point and the expected voltage (computed assuming
nominal geometry) manifests as a systematic additive offset. This is distinct from
random noise: it is a fixed bias for a given electrode configuration that persists
across all measurements within a session (Kolehmainen et al., 1997; Barber & Brown,
1988). The error is structured—each electrode contributes its own constant offset
to all measurements it participates in—producing block-correlated bias in the
measurement vector.

**Justification for inclusion in the noise model**: The EIDORS forward model
computes voltages on a mesh with electrode positions defined exactly at construction
time. It has no mechanism to represent uncertainty in electrode location. In a
full-physics approach, one could perturb the electrode node positions on the mesh
and re-run the forward solve; however, this requires mesh regeneration or node
repositioning and a new FEM assembly for every sample—computationally infeasible
for large-scale dataset generation. For small displacements, the voltage error is
approximately linear in the positional offset and manifests as a per-electrode
additive bias (Kolehmainen et al., 1997, §4). The uniform-distribution model used
here is a first-order approximation of this effect that can be applied post-hoc
without modifying the forward model, enabling ablation studies at experiment time.

**Mathematical formulation**:
```
Δv'_i = Δv_i + b_e(i)
b_e ~ U(-b_max, +b_max)
```

where `e(i)` maps measurement `i` to the electrode responsible for that measurement
block. Each electrode has a random fixed bias.

**Parameters**:
| Parameter | Default | Range | Source |
|-----------|---------|-------|--------|
| b_max | 0.02 | 0.01–0.05 | Kolehmainen et al. (1997) |
| n_electrodes | 16 | — | System configuration |

**Notes**: Applied per-electrode with random offsets (rather than a smooth spatial
gradient across the array). This correctly models that each electrode site has its
own independent positioning error. The per-electrode structure produces
block-structured bias in the measurement vector, consistent with the observation
that positioning errors introduce systematic (not random) distortion patterns
(Barber & Brown, 1988). The uniform distribution is chosen as a maximum-entropy
prior given only the bound constraint (no preferred direction of displacement),
following the approach of Kolehmainen et al. (1997) who demonstrated that even
modest positioning errors (2–5% of electrode spacing) can produce reconstruction
artefacts comparable in magnitude to the target conductivity change.

### 4. Quantisation Noise

**Physical origin**: All EIT systems digitise analogue voltage measurements via an
analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) prior to digital processing. The ADC maps a
continuous voltage range onto a finite set of discrete levels, introducing an
irreversible rounding error on every sample. This quantisation error arises from
the fundamental resolution limitation of the converter:

- **Finite bit-depth**: An $n$-bit ADC divides its full-scale voltage range
  $V_\text{range}$ into $2^n$ discrete levels, each separated by one least
  significant bit (LSB = $V_\text{range} / 2^n$). Any voltage falling between
  two adjacent levels is rounded to the nearest, incurring an error of at most
  $\pm \text{LSB}/2$ (Kester, 2005, Ch. 2).
- **Uniform error distribution**: For signals spanning many quantisation levels
  (the typical operating regime), the quantisation error is well-modelled as
  uniformly distributed over $[-\text{LSB}/2, +\text{LSB}/2]$, independent across
  samples (Widrow & Kollár, 2008, §4.3). This approximation holds when the input
  signal varies by more than a few LSBs between successive samples.
- **Effective resolution degradation**: In practice, ADC performance is further
  limited by differential and integral non-linearity (DNL, INL), aperture jitter,
  and internal noise, which reduce the effective number of bits (ENOB) below the
  nominal resolution (Kester, 2005, Ch. 5). A 16-bit ADC may achieve only 14–15
  effective bits under typical operating conditions.

For a 16-bit ADC with $V_\text{range} = 1$ V, the LSB is approximately 15.3 µV.
This is typically the smallest noise component in a well-designed EIT system, but
becomes significant for low-cost portable systems employing 12-bit converters
(LSB ≈ 244 µV) or when operating at high gain settings that compress the signal
dynamic range relative to the ADC full-scale.

**Justification for inclusion in the noise model**: The EIDORS forward solver
operates in double-precision floating point (approximately 15–16 significant
decimal digits), producing voltage values with effectively infinite resolution
relative to the physical measurement. Real hardware truncates these values to the
ADC's bit-depth. Although quantisation noise is typically dominated by other
components at 16-bit resolution, its inclusion serves two purposes: (i) it
completes the physical signal chain model for systems where low-resolution ADCs are
employed (e.g., cost-constrained wearable devices), and (ii) it enables severity
sweep experiments to characterise classifier robustness under degraded digitisation
conditions, relevant to hardware design trade-off studies (Holder, 2004, §3.2).

**Mathematical formulation**:
```
Δv'_i = Δv_i + q_i
q_i ~ U(-LSB/2, +LSB/2)
LSB = V_range / 2^n_bits
```

**Parameters**:
| Parameter | Default | Range | Source |
|-----------|---------|-------|--------|
| n_bits | 16 | 12–24 | EIT hardware specs |
| V_range (V) | 1.0 | 0.5–5.0 | Typical ADC full-scale |

**Notes**: At 16-bit resolution, LSB ≈ 15.3 µV — typically the smallest component
in the noise budget. The RMS quantisation noise is $\text{LSB} / \sqrt{12}$ ≈ 4.4 µV,
well below typical Gaussian measurement noise floors. Becomes significant only for
low-resolution portable systems (12-bit: LSB ≈ 244 µV) or in severity sweeps where
the effective bit-depth is artificially reduced to stress-test classifier robustness.

## Ablation Study Design

The noise model enables three ablation configurations:

### Single-Component (4 experiments)
Each component enabled individually — reveals standalone contribution:
- `only_gaussian`, `only_contact_impedance`, `only_electrode_bias`, `only_quantisation`

### Leave-One-Out (4 experiments)
All components except one — reveals marginal value when others present:
- `without_gaussian`, `without_contact_impedance`, `without_electrode_bias`, `without_quantisation`

### Severity Sweep (7 levels)
All parameters scaled by multiplier {0.0×, 0.5×, 1.0×, 1.5×, 2.0×, 2.5×, 3.0×}:
- Tests generalisation beyond training-time noise intensity
- Produces robustness curves (accuracy vs. severity)
- 0.0× provides a clean baseline for reference
- For Gaussian noise, severity adjusts effective SNR: `effective_snr = snr_db + 20·log₁₀(1/severity)`
- For other components, severity linearly scales the parameter magnitude

### Multi-Severity Domain Randomisation
To address over-specialisation at training noise level:
- Sample severity uniformly from a range (default: [0.5, 2.0]) per mini-batch
- Each batch sees a different noise intensity during training
- Produces noise-invariant features rather than noise-level-specific features
- Configured via `noise_augmentation.severity_range` in `config.yaml`

## Configuration

Parameters are specified in [`matlab/configs/noise_params.yaml`](../matlab/configs/noise_params.yaml).
Each component has an `enabled` toggle for ablation control.

To generate a clean dataset:
```yaml
enabled: false  # Master toggle disables all components
```

To run single-component ablation:
```yaml
enabled: true
gaussian:
  enabled: true
contact_impedance:
  enabled: false
electrode_bias:
  enabled: false
quantisation:
  enabled: false
```

## Implementation

- **MATLAB**: [`matlab/noise_model/add_noise.m`](../matlab/noise_model/add_noise.m) — applies noise during dataset generation
- **Python (noise module)**: [`src/eit_sim2real/data/noise.py`](../src/eit_sim2real/data/noise.py) — full 4-component noise model for on-the-fly augmentation during training and ablation
- **Python (training)**: [`src/eit_sim2real/train.py`](../src/eit_sim2real/train.py) — online noise augmentation with optional multi-severity domain randomisation
- **Python (ablation)**: [`src/eit_sim2real/experiments/ablation.py`](../src/eit_sim2real/experiments/ablation.py) — systematic ablation via Python-side noise injection (no MATLAB dependency at experiment time)
- **Python (evaluation)**: [`src/eit_sim2real/evaluate.py`](../src/eit_sim2real/evaluate.py) — `evaluate_severity_sweep()` generates fresh noise at each severity level

The Python noise module (`NoiseConfig` class) provides factory methods for ablation:
- `NoiseConfig.only('gaussian')` — single-component experiment
- `NoiseConfig.without('contact_impedance')` — leave-one-out experiment
- `NoiseConfig.all_off()` — clean baseline
- `NoiseConfig(severity=2.0)` — severity-scaled noise
