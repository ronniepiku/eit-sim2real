\section{EIT Simulation Framework}
\label{sec:method-simulation}

\subsection{Forward Model and Mesh Configuration}
\label{sec:method-mesh}

The \gls{eit} forward problem is solved using EIDORS v3.12-ng \parencite{adler2006}, computing boundary voltages from a specified conductivity distribution via the \gls{fem}. The governing equation is the generalised Laplace equation (Equation~\ref{eq:eit-forward} in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}), solved with Neumann boundary conditions and the Complete Electrode Model (CEM) to represent finite electrode area and contact impedance \parencite{cheney1999, somersalo1992}.

The domain is a 2D circular cross-section representing a transverse slice of a prosthetic forearm sensor, discretised using \texttt{mk\_common\_model('d2C', 16)}. This canonical EIDORS reference family was preferred over a custom mesh so that methodological contributions are not confounded with bespoke meshing choices \parencite{adler2006, adler2009}, and 2D was retained for tractability at dataset scale (25,000 forward solves per mesh) and consistency with prior \gls{eit} classification work.


Mesh selection was evidence-based rather than assumed: three matched noisy datasets were generated at coarse, medium and fine refinement and a CNN trained per mesh was cross-evaluated on all three. Mesh \texttt{d} (medium) was retained, transferring more stably across meshes (4.37\,pp cross-mesh range) than the coarse alternative (8.35\,pp) at comparable runtime, so the choice reflects generalisation rather than cost. Full cross-mesh figures and compute profiles are in Appendix~\ref{app:mesh}.

\subsection{Material Model: Ionic Hydrogel}
\label{sec:method-material}

The simulated substrate models an ionic hydrogel layer, following the material system demonstrated by \textcite{park2022skin} and characterised by \textcite{costacornella2023}, exhibiting the positive piezoresistive response described in Section~\ref{sec:lit-eskin-materials} and the multilayer structure of Figure~\ref{fig:park-hydrogel-structure}.

Hydrogel was preferred to the more durable conductive elastomers of Table~\ref{tab:material-comparison} for two reasons: it is the substrate used by the most directly comparable \gls{eit} e-skin studies \parencite{park2022skin, costacornella2023, jiang2024fista}, and \textcite{costacornella2023} provides a piezoresistive characterisation in an \gls{eit} configuration that no equivalent elastomer study offers.

The forward model assigns conductivity $\sigma < \sigma_0$ within the contact region, decreasing with applied pressure. Background conductivity is normalised to $\sigma_0 = 1.0$\,S/m, standard in EIDORS-based studies \parencite{adler2006}; this does not affect the voltage \emph{difference} vectors used for classification, which depend only on the contrast ratio $\sigma / \sigma_0$.

\subsection{Touch Class Definitions}
\label{sec:method-classes}

Five touch classes represent the principal interaction modalities encountered in prosthetic use \parencite{park2022skin, silvera-tawil2012eit}, each parameterised by a conductivity range ($\sigma$) and a spatial radius ($r$), with random position constrained to the inner 40\% of the mesh (Table~\ref{tab:class-params}).

\centering
\caption[Touch class parameterisation]{Touch class parameterisation. Conductivity and radius ranges are sampled uniformly for each generated sample. $\Delta\sigma$ denotes the percentage decrease from the homogeneous baseline.}
\label{tab:class-params}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{$\sigma$ (S/m)} & \textbf{$\Delta\sigma$} & \textbf{Radius} \\
\midrule
No contact   & 1.00         & 0\%      & --- \\
Light touch  & [0.85, 0.95] & 5--15\%  & [0.06, 0.10] \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three design principles govern this parameterisation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sign constraint.} All contact classes satisfy $\sigma < \sigma_0$, consistent with ionic hydrogels' positive piezoresistive response \parencite{costacornella2023}.

    \item \textbf{Pressure-driven conductivity change.} The conductivity drop is proportional to local pressure $P = F/(\pi r^2)$ rather than force alone, which separates the classes physically: point contact (small area, high pressure) produces the largest per-element $\Delta\sigma$ and distributed contact (large area, low pressure) the smallest. Point and distributed contacts therefore have comparable signal energy but different spatial distributions.

    \item \textbf{Contact geometry.} Radii follow Hertzian contact mechanics for soft elastomers: for $E^* \approx 150$\,kPa \parencite{kim2024} and forces of 0.1--4\,N, predicted radii span approximately 2--25\,mm on the unit-normalised mesh (Table~\ref{tab:class-params}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Position constraint.} Contact positions are restricted to $|x|, |y| \leq 0.4$ on the unit disc, since \gls{eit} sensitivity is far greater near the boundary electrodes than at the centre \parencite{adler2006}, and the prosthetic's sensing surface is the top of the sensor, not the electrode boundary. An earlier, wider constraint ($|x|, |y| \leq 0.7$) let within-class positional variation dominate over between-class differences, motivating the tighter bound.

Conductivity magnitudes are \emph{physics-informed priors} rather than measured calibration constants, since no published study provides a complete gauge-factor calibration for the ionic hydrogel under these conditions \parencite{costacornella2023}.

Each sample is the voltage difference vector $\Delta \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{contact}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{baseline}} \in \mathbb{R}^{208}$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:lit-eit-patterns}, where $\mathbf{v}_{\text{baseline}}$ is the homogeneous no-touch reference. Differencing removes the large common-mode baseline and mirrors real \gls{eit} operation, which acquires a reference frame during calibration \parencite{adler2006}.

\section{Noise Model}
\label{sec:method-noise}

The noise model comprises four independently configurable components, each capturing a distinct physical degradation source documented in the experimental \gls{eit} literature.

\subsection{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:method-noise-rationale}

Real \gls{eit} hardware corrupts measurements through multiple concurrent, statistically distinct processes \parencite{adler2006, vilhunen2002, boone1996}, whereas prior work models at most one, invariably Gaussian noise \parencite{chen2023, park2022skin}. Training with all four dominant per-frame distortions active simultaneously is termed the \emph{foundational mode}: the classifier is exposed to the co-occurring errors expected at deployment.

Temporal drift is deliberately excluded, being a \emph{sequential} phenomenon that cannot be represented within a single static frame; modelling it as a spatial random walk across channels would introduce artefacts with no hardware counterpart. Addressing it requires sequential data (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}).

\subsection{Component Specifications}
\label{sec:method-noise-components}

All components operate on the voltage difference vector $\Delta\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{208}$ and are applied sequentially in a fixed order (Gaussian $\to$ contact impedance $\to$ bias $\to$ quantisation) reflecting the physical signal chain: measurement noise enters at the analogue front-end, followed by electrode-level effects, geometric bias, and finally digitisation. The contact-impedance and electrode-bias components are applied \emph{per-electrode} rather than per-measurement, since these are properties of the interface. Each of the 16 electrodes carries a single value mapped to blocks of the measurement vector, producing the block-structured distortion real hardware exhibits. Unless stated otherwise, every parameter magnitude below is a nominal design point rather than a measured calibration constant, bounded by the explicit sensitivity sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:method-noise-sensitivity} and summarised in Table~\ref{tab:noise-params}.

\paragraph{Component 1: Gaussian measurement noise.} Models the aggregate effect of thermal noise, amplifier noise, and electromagnetic interference in the analogue measurement chain \parencite{adler2006, boone1996}.

\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf{v}' = \Delta\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{n}, \quad \mathbf{n} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}), \quad \sigma_n = \begin{cases} \frac{\|\Delta\mathbf{v}\|_2}{\|\hat{\mathbf{n}}\|_2} \cdot 10^{-\text{SNR}/20} & \text{if } \|\Delta\mathbf{v}\| > 0 \\ \frac{n_{\text{floor}} \cdot N}{\|\hat{\mathbf{n}}\|_2} & \text{if } \|\Delta\mathbf{v}\| = 0 \end{cases}
\label{eq:noise-gaussian}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{\mathbf{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and $N = 208$ (SNR and noise-floor values in Table~\ref{tab:noise-params}). No agreed instrumentation figure exists, published \gls{eit} studies adding noise anywhere between 10 and 65\,dB \parencite{chen2023, dong2025lattice}, so 40\,dB is taken as a mid-range value. The noise floor is essential, as without it, the no-contact class remains an exact zero vector, a deterministic cluster with no hardware counterpart.

\paragraph{Component 2: Electrode contact impedance variation.} Models imperfect and spatially varying electrode--substrate contact, producing multiplicative per-electrode distortion.

\begin{equation}
\Delta v'_i = \Delta v_i \cdot z_{e(i)}, \quad z_e = \exp(\epsilon_e), \quad \epsilon_e \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma_z^2), \quad \sigma_z = 0.10
\label{eq:noise-impedance}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $e(i)$ maps measurement $i$ to its electrode block ($e \in \{1, \ldots, 16\}$), so each electrode contributes a single factor to all measurements it participates in, producing block-structured multiplicative distortion. The log-normal form guarantees $z_e > 0$ and yields asymmetric variation about unity. \textcite{vilhunen2002} show contact impedance cannot be neglected in static imaging and must be estimated jointly with the interior admittivity, motivating its explicit inclusion here.

This default is the least well-constrained parameter in the model: no published study provides electrode--hydrogel contact-impedance \emph{variation} data suitable for direct parameterisation \parencite{costacornella2023, park2022skin}, though the physical analogy of a metal electrode contacting an ionic conductor supports the same order of magnitude.

\paragraph{Component 3: Electrode positioning bias.} Models systematic measurement error from imprecise electrode placement on the physical substrate.

\begin{equation}
\Delta v'_i = \Delta v_i + b_{e(i)}, \quad b_e \sim \mathcal{U}(-b_{\max},\, b_{\max}), \quad b_{\max} = 0.02
\label{eq:noise-bias}
\end{equation}

\noindent where each electrode receives an independently drawn fixed offset, reflecting that placement error at one site is unrelated to the others, and producing block-structured additive bias. \textcite{kolehmainen1997} identify electrode placement as a dominant modelling error in static imaging, and an independent per-electrode draw captures its block structure more faithfully than a linear gradient across measurements. The independence assumption is revisited as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations}, since this component proves to dominate the domain gap.

\paragraph{Component 4: Quantisation noise.} Models the finite precision of the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) as additive uniform noise bounded by half the least-significant bit, the standard statistical model for a mid-tread uniform quantiser operating well above its noise floor \parencite{widrow1996quantization}.

\begin{equation}
\Delta v'_i = \Delta v_i + q_i, \quad q_i \sim \mathcal{U}\left(-\frac{\text{LSB}}{2},\, \frac{\text{LSB}}{2}\right), \quad \text{LSB} = \frac{V_{\text{range}}}{2^{n_{\text{bits}}}}
\label{eq:noise-quant}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $n_{\text{bits}} = 16$ and $V_{\text{range}} = 1.0$\,V (LSB~$\approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$\,V), corresponding to a typical \gls{eit} data-acquisition specification and setting the minimum noise floor of the digital chain.

\section{Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:method-dataset}

\subsection{Sampling Procedure}
\label{sec:method-dataset-sampling}

The dataset comprises 25{,}000 samples (5{,}000 per class), generated using the MATLAB pipeline with a fixed seed ($\texttt{seed} = 42$) for reproducibility. For each sample:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Select the target class and sample conductivity $\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}[\sigma_{\min}, \sigma_{\max}]$, radius $r \sim \mathcal{U}[r_{\min}, r_{\max}]$, and position $(x, y) \sim \mathcal{U}[-0.4, 0.4]^2$.
    \item Construct the inhomogeneous conductivity image: $\sigma(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma$ for elements within radius $r$ of $(x, y)$; $\sigma_0$ elsewhere.
    \item Solve the forward problem to obtain $\mathbf{v}_{\text{contact}}$.
    \item Compute $\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\text{clean}} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{contact}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{baseline}}$.
    \item Apply the full noise model to produce $\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\text{noisy}}$.
    \item Store both clean and noisy vectors with the class label and generation metadata.
\end{enumerate}

Storing both vectors enables the four-condition evaluation matrix (Section~\ref{sec:method-eval-conditions}) without regenerating data. Dataset size was set empirically: a size sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-size-learning-curve}) shows noise-aware accuracy plateauing near $10^4$ samples, so 25{,}000 sits beyond the plateau within a practical generation budget, while the clean-trained curve remains flat at chance throughout, an early sign that no quantity of clean data addresses the domain shift.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\subsection{Train/Validation/Test Split}
\label{sec:method-splits}

The dataset is partitioned 70/15/15 into training, validation and test sets by stratified random sampling, preserving class proportions across all three. A fixed random state ($\texttt{seed} = 42$) ensures all models and conditions are evaluated on identical samples.

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:method-eda}

Preprocessing decisions were determined empirically using a \texttt{RepeatedStratifiedKFold} ($k=5$, $n_{\text{repeats}}=3$) protocol yielding 15 paired evaluations per pipeline, with supporting tables in Appendix~\ref{app:eda}.

Features are normalised with \texttt{RobustScaler} (median subtraction, interquartile-range scaling), fitted \emph{exclusively} on the training split to prevent leakage. It was preferred over \texttt{StandardScaler} empirically, achieving macro-F1 $0.718 \pm 0.004$ against $0.713 \pm 0.004$. Where noise is injected on the Python side, perturbations are applied in raw voltage space and re-transformed with the training-fitted scaler, keeping the noise model's physical interpretation intact.

The full 208-dimensional vector is retained. Two reductions were tested and rejected: correlation pruning at $\rho = 0.75$ cost 3.5\,pp macro-F1 and \gls{pca} at 95.3\% variance cost 4.5\,pp, each ranking below the full-feature pipeline in all 15 folds. Aggressive reduction destroys the local structure the convolutional filters exploit, and Appendix~\ref{app:feature-reduction} shows this matters far more under noise than these clean-data margins suggest.

\section{Model Architectures}
\label{sec:method-models}

Three classical baselines and a purpose-designed 1D-\gls{cnn} are evaluated under identical data conditions, addressing the gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:lit-ml-concerns}: an \gls{svm} \parencite{cortes1995}, an \gls{rf} \parencite{breiman2001}, and an \gls{mlp} sized to match the \gls{cnn} head's fully connected capacity, isolating the effect of convolutional feature extraction (full configurations in Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}).

\section{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:method-training}

\subsection{CNN Training}
\label{sec:method-training-cnn}

The CNN is trained using the Adam optimiser \parencite{kingma2015adam} with the hyperparameters in Table~\ref{tab:cnn-hyperparams}. Two safeguards, with values set experimentally, prevent overfitting: ReduceLROnPlateau and early stopping \parencite{prechelt1998early}, always retaining the best model.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

\label{tab:cnn-hyperparams}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule

Maximum epochs & 200 \\

For noise-augmented training, label smoothing \parencite{muller2019labelsmooth} and increased dropout \parencite{srivastava2014dropout} add further regularisation. Online domain randomisation \parencite{tobin2017} samples fresh noise draws each batch, applied in raw voltage space before re-scaling into the model input, preserving the noise model's physical interpretation.

The loss function is cross-entropy over the five classes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{5} y_{i,c} \log\left(\text{softmax}(\hat{y}_{i,c})\right)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $y_{i,c}$ is the one-hot encoded true label and $\hat{y}_{i,c}$ are the raw logits, suiting single-label multi-class classification and penalising confident misclassification strongly.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection}
\label{sec:method-training-baselines}

Baseline models are trained on the same training split using scikit-learn \parencite{cortes1995, breiman2001}. The \gls{mlp} uses its own internal early stopping, while the \gls{svm} and \gls{rf} models are trained to convergence.

Hyperparameters for all four families are fixed \emph{a priori} rather than tuned per condition, following canonical references for each family \parencite{cortes1995, breiman2001, lecun1998efficient}. A condition-specific search would confound the noise-augmentation effect with capacity and regularisation changes, and bias comparisons toward whichever condition received the larger search budget \parencite{caruana2006empirical, henderson2018reproducibility}. To confirm the augmentation benefit is not a hyperparameter artefact, learning rate, dropout \parencite{srivastava2014dropout} and weight decay are swept separately, each retrained from scratch (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn-hyperparameter-sensitivity}).

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:method-reproducibility}

All experiments use fixed random seeds: NumPy, PyTorch and MATLAB all seeded at 42. The complete configuration is stored in a YAML file, enabling exact reproduction from a single command.

\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:method-eval}

\subsection{Evaluation Conditions}
\label{sec:method-eval-conditions}

To quantify the effect of noise augmentation, all models are evaluated under four conditions forming a $2 \times 2$ matrix:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

\label{tab:eval-conditions}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}

Noisy$\to$Noisy is the operationally relevant cell, since every real measurement carries hardware noise, and the augmentation benefit is read as the improvement from Clean$\to$Noisy to Noisy$\to$Noisy. Domain adaptation methods \parencite{ganin2016, tzeng2017adda} are not used as comparators, since they require unlabelled target-domain samples a simulation-only study cannot supply.


\subsection{Performance Metrics}
\label{sec:method-metrics}

Two metrics are reported: overall accuracy and macro-averaged F1, which weights all classes equally and is more informative given the classes' differing signal magnitudes. Per-class precision, recall and F1 appear in confusion matrices. Headline values are computed on the held-out test split; validation metrics serve only for scheduling, early stopping and model selection.

\subsection{Validation Protocol}
\label{sec:method-stats}

Each experiment is repeated over 5 independent random seeds with stratified 70/15/15 splits \parencite{kohavi1995crossval}, giving the mean $\pm$ standard deviation reported throughout. Seeds vary both the data partition and weight initialisation, so the reported spread bounds their combined effect, and models are retrained from scratch each time. A validation slice is carved from the training partition within every run, so early stopping never observes held-out data.

\subsection{Robustness Evaluation}
\label{sec:method-robustness}


Following the multi-severity paradigm of \textcite{hendrycks2019}, trained models are evaluated at severity multipliers from $0.0\times$ to $3.0\times$ in steps of $0.5\times$, applied to all noise parameters simultaneously for each training regime. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} extracts three quantities: degradation from training severity to $3\times$, the \emph{crossover severity} at which randomisation overtakes fixed-noise training, and each regime's clean-domain accuracy.


\subsection{Ablation Protocol}
\label{sec:method-ablation}

The ablation isolates the contribution of each noise component, and the sensitivity of the conclusions to component ordering through four complementary designs:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Core mismatch conditions}: the four-condition matrix instantiated within the ablation pipeline, for direct comparison against the variants.

    \item \textbf{Single-component training}: four experiments, each with only one component active.

    \item \textbf{Exhaustive subset ablation}: all $2^4 - 1 = 15$ non-empty subsets, exposing interactions that marginal single-component analysis cannot capture.

    \item \textbf{Component-order ablation}: all physically admissible orderings within each subset, with quantisation last (digitisation terminates the chain) and contact impedance before electrode bias (multiplicative precedes additive).
\end{enumerate}

Every configuration is scored twice, since the two protocols answer different questions and, as Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows, give opposite answers. \emph{Matched} evaluation tests a model on data corrupted by the same components it was trained with, measuring the difficulty of a world containing only those components. \emph{Deployment} evaluation tests it against the full four-component corruption, measuring what omitting components costs in the field.

Only the second is operationally meaningful, since hardware exhibits all four sources simultaneously. Matched evaluation silently adjusts the test distribution to suit the model, rewarding configurations that define an easier problem rather than those that transfer. It is retained here only as a diagnostic.

Throughout, corruption is applied in \emph{foundational mode}: noise is drawn once per training sample and held fixed across epochs, as the pre-generated dataset behaves. This prevents the training regime and the active component set from varying together. All variants inherit the same regularisation preset as the main noise-trained condition, leaving the component set as the only difference.
