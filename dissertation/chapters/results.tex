\chapter{Findings and Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experimental findings against the objectives of Section~\ref{sec:intro-aims}. Section~\ref{sec:res-main} establishes the simulation-to-reality gap and the extent to which noise-aware training closes it. Section~\ref{sec:res-features} examines feature reduction. Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} presents the noise-component ablation, which identifies the minimal noise model needed for deployable robustness. Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness} evaluates robustness beyond the training noise level, and Section~\ref{sec:res-extended} reports the supporting analyses: statistical significance, per-class vulnerability, calibration, domain characterisation and sensitivity.

Unless stated otherwise, headline values are held-out test metrics reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation over five independent seeds with stratified 70/15/15 splits. Significance testing uses a separate stratified 5-fold cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:method-stats}), so that paired comparisons are made on disjoint partitions rather than on overlapping random splits.

\label{sec:res-main}

\begin{table}[htbp]

\label{tab:main-results-raw}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Clean$\to$Clean} & \textbf{Clean$\to$Noisy} & \textbf{Noisy$\to$Noisy} & \textbf{Noisy$\to$Clean} \\
\midrule
CNN  & 94.00 $\pm$ 6.89 & 19.98 $\pm$ 0.33 & \textbf{75.81 $\pm$ 0.62} & \textbf{52.23 $\pm$ 1.94} \\
MLP  & \textbf{98.81 $\pm$ 0.69} & 21.32 $\pm$ 0.34 & 57.25 $\pm$ 0.80 & 47.61 $\pm$ 0.69 \\
RF   & 91.03 $\pm$ 0.30 & \textbf{22.57 $\pm$ 0.18} & 57.85 $\pm$ 0.64 & 29.91 $\pm$ 3.00 \\
SVM  & 83.38 $\pm$ 0.43 & 20.00 $\pm$ 0.00 & 50.90 $\pm$ 0.72 & 51.66 $\pm$ 0.41 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three findings follow. First, every model achieves strong clean-domain accuracy (83--99\%), confirming that the forward model produces class representations separable by both classical and deep approaches; the task itself is not the limiting factor. Second, \emph{every} model collapses to approximately chance ($\approx$20\%) under Clean$\to$Noisy. The collapse is architecture-independent and feature-representation-independent (Section~\ref{sec:res-features}), which identifies the gap as \emph{distributional} rather than a deficit of model capacity: no classifier can compensate at inference for a shift it never encountered during training. This is considerably more extreme than the corruption-induced degradations of up to 40\% reported for image classifiers by \textcite{hendrycks2019}.

Third, noise-aware training recovers a large fraction of the loss. The CNN reaches 75.81\% under Noisy$\to$Noisy, an improvement of 55.8 percentage points (pp) over the same architecture trained on clean data. The effect size is extreme because the two conditions are almost perfectly separated with small within-condition variance; it confirms the direction and reliability of the effect, but is not a meaningful measure of its practical magnitude.

The CNN outperforms the strongest baseline on noisy data by 17.9\,pp (RF, 57.85\%), consistent with the convolutional inductive bias being able to exploit the block-structured, per-electrode form of the dominant corruption (Section~\ref{sec:res-domain}), which fully-connected and kernel methods treating the 208 measurements exchangeably cannot represent as compactly. On clean data, the ranking inverts: the MLP is best (98.81\%) while the CNN both underperforms it and is markedly unstable (std 6.89\%, per-seed range 83.4--99.4\%).

\subsection{Training Regimes}
\label{sec:res-regimes}

Table~\ref{tab:augmented-results} compares the three noise-aware training regimes for the CNN.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{CNN accuracy (\%) by training regime on raw features. Mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 5 seeds.}
\label{tab:augmented-results}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Training Regime} & \textbf{Eval Clean} & \textbf{Eval Noisy} \\
\midrule
Fixed noisy dataset            & 52.23 $\pm$ 1.94 & \textbf{75.81 $\pm$ 0.62} \\
Online augmentation            & 40.17 $\pm$ 2.12 & 69.50 $\pm$ 0.62 \\
Mixed (30\% clean + augmented) & \textbf{71.50 $\pm$ 0.42} & 69.17 $\pm$ 0.68 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Training on the fixed, pre-generated noisy dataset gives the highest noisy-domain accuracy (75.81\%), exceeding online augmentation by 6.3\,pp ($t = 15.37$, $p_{\text{corr}} = 5.2\times10^{-4}$, $d = 6.87$). Both are far above the clean-trained baseline ($p_{\text{corr}} < 10^{-6}$ in each case). The advantage of fixed-noise training is, however, specific to the severity it was trained at, and reverses at higher severities (Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}).

The regimes differ sharply in what they sacrifice. Fixed-noise and online-augmented training both lose heavily on clean data (52.2\% and 40.2\%), because neither ever observes an uncorrupted measurement: the no-contact class in particular is seen only in perturbed form, so clean zero-vectors fall outside the learned input distribution. Mixed training, retaining 30\% clean samples per batch, recovers clean-domain accuracy to 71.50\% while giving up only 0.3\,pp of noisy-domain accuracy relative to online augmentation. Where the deployment noise level is uncertain, or a device may be operated shortly after calibration, mixed training is therefore better balanced despite not being best in any single cell.

\small

\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Raw (208-D)} & \textbf{PCA (7-D)} & \textbf{LDA (4-D)} \\
\midrule

Clean$\to$Noisy & 22.57 (RF)  & 24.43 (RF)  & 20.10 (RF) \\

On clean data both reductions are competitive: LDA retains 91.43\% with only four dimensions, confirming that the class-discriminative structure is genuinely low-dimensional. Under noise they are actively harmful, halving Noisy$\to$Noisy accuracy from 75.81\% to $\approx$38\%. The projections are estimated from the covariance of \emph{clean} data and therefore retain only the directions separating classes in the absence of noise; the dominant corruption acts along different directions (Section~\ref{sec:res-domain}), so the information needed to discount it is discarded before training begins. This is a concrete instance of the result of \textcite{ben-david2010domain} that minimising source-domain error is insufficient when the target distribution differs: a representation optimal for the source can be pathological for the target. Practically, it argues against dimensionality reduction as a preprocessing step for any EIT system intended for deployment, and justifies retaining the full 208-dimensional vector (Section~\ref{sec:method-eda}).

\section{Noise Component Ablation}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

The ablation asks which noise components a training-time model must include. This requires care in how each configuration is evaluated. A model trained with only component $X$ can be tested either on data corrupted by $X$ alone (\emph{matched}), or on data carrying the full four-component corruption (\emph{deployment}). Only the latter is operationally meaningful: real hardware exhibits all error sources simultaneously, so a training noise model that omits a component must nonetheless face that component in the field. Both are reported, because their disagreement is itself the principal finding.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Single-component ablation. Each CNN is trained with one noise component active and evaluated twice: on the same single component (matched) and on the full four-component corruption (deployment). Mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 5 seeds.}
\label{tab:ablation-single}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Training noise} & \textbf{Matched (\%)} & \textbf{Deployment (\%)} \\
\midrule
Gaussian only           & 98.90 $\pm$ 0.19 & 19.75 $\pm$ 0.80 \\
Contact impedance only  & 96.74 $\pm$ 2.44 & 19.05 $\pm$ 0.91 \\
Electrode bias only     & 68.21 $\pm$ 0.78 & \textbf{56.69 $\pm$ 3.06} \\
Quantisation only       & 97.29 $\pm$ 3.18 & 19.51 $\pm$ 0.34 \\
\midrule
Full four-component     & \multicolumn{2}{c}{68.98} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The two columns rank the components in opposite orders, and the matched column is misleading. Judged on matched evaluation, electrode bias appears the \emph{most} damaging component (68.21\%, lowest of the four) while Gaussian noise appears benign (98.90\%). Judged on deployment evaluation the conclusion reverses entirely: models trained on Gaussian noise, contact impedance or quantisation alone all perform at chance (19.05--19.75\%), whereas the electrode-bias model retains 56.69\%. The matched column measures the difficulty of an artificial world containing a single corruption, not the value of modelling that corruption. Reporting it alone---the conventional practice in the studies reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-ml-concerns}---would invert the design guidance derived from it.

Two complementary claims follow. Electrode bias is \emph{necessary}: every configuration omitting it collapses to chance under realistic conditions, irrespective of how well it scores in its own matched world. Electrode bias alone is \emph{not sufficient}: at 56.69\% it falls 12.3\,pp short of the full model. Robust deployment therefore requires the dominant structured error to be modelled jointly with the remaining sources, which is the empirical justification for training on all error sources simultaneously rather than sequentially.

A two-component model consisting of Gaussian noise and electrode bias reaches 69.25\%, statistically indistinguishable from the best three-component subset (69.39\%) and from the full four-component model (68.98\%). Contact impedance and quantisation contribute no measurable deployable robustness once these two are present.

Each required component has a distinct, identifiable role. Electrode bias supplies the dominant structured distortion that defines the domain shift (Section~\ref{sec:res-domain}). Gaussian noise supplies the measurement noise floor, which matters disproportionately for the no-contact class: without it, no-contact samples are exact zero vectors in training but carry noise at test time, so the class becomes undetectable. The per-component severity sweep isolates this mechanism directly---enabling Gaussian noise at any non-zero severity raises accuracy from 45.0\% to 65.1\%, a gain of 20.1\,pp corresponding almost exactly to the 20\% of test samples belonging to the no-contact class (Figure~\ref{fig:severity-sweep}).

\subsection{Sensitivity to Component Ordering}
\label{sec:res-ablation-order}

Because the components are applied compositionally, their order can alter the resulting distribution. For the full four-component model the effect is modest (0.95\,pp between the best and worst admissible ordering, 69.39\% versus 68.43\%). Within two-component subsets it is substantial: Gaussian~$\to$~electrode bias achieves 69.25\%, whereas electrode bias~$\to$~Gaussian achieves only 58.21\%---a difference of 11.0\,pp for the same two components.

This follows from the Gaussian component being specified relative to signal amplitude (Equation~\ref{eq:noise-gaussian}) rather than as an absolute magnitude. Applied first, its scale is set by the clean voltage difference; applied after the bias, its scale is set by signal-plus-bias, which for these data is roughly forty times larger (Section~\ref{sec:res-domain}). The later ordering therefore injects far more Gaussian energy and produces a training distribution matching deployment less closely. The effect is an artefact of the parameterisation rather than of the physics, but it is a practical caution for compositional noise models: where any component is defined relative to the running signal, ordering is not a free choice, and the physically-motivated signal-chain order adopted in Section~\ref{sec:method-noise-components} should be preserved.

\section{Robustness Under Severity Scaling}
\label{sec:res-robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:noise-type-sweep} evaluates each training regime across noise severities from 0$\times$ (clean) to 3$\times$ the calibrated level.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

Fixed-noise training peaks at its training severity and degrades steadily beyond it; randomised regimes are substantially flatter and overtake it at approximately 2.2$\times$.}
\label{fig:noise-type-sweep}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Accuracy (\%) against noise severity multiplier by training regime.}
\label{tab:severity-sweep}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Regime} & \textbf{0$\times$} & \textbf{0.5$\times$} & \textbf{1$\times$} & \textbf{1.5$\times$} & \textbf{2$\times$} & \textbf{2.5$\times$} & \textbf{3$\times$} \\
\midrule
Fixed noisy         & 55.15 & 75.52 & \textbf{75.95} & 73.95 & \textbf{70.75} & 66.32 & 60.99 \\
Online augmentation & 41.44 & 67.60 & 68.91 & 68.96 & 68.67 & \textbf{68.19} & \textbf{67.55} \\
Mixed               & \textbf{70.40} & 69.25 & 69.79 & 69.79 & 69.25 & 68.88 & 68.35 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The regimes exhibit a clear trade-off between peak accuracy and breadth of robustness (Table~\ref{tab:severity-sweep}). Fixed-noise training is best at the severity it was trained on (75.95\% at 1$\times$) but degrades monotonically thereafter, losing 15.0\,pp by 3$\times$. The randomised regimes, trained across severities sampled from $[0.5, 2.0]$, are markedly flatter: online augmentation varies by only 1.4\,pp between 1$\times$ and 3$\times$. The curves cross at approximately 2.2$\times$, beyond which randomisation is superior.

This is the behaviour domain randomisation predicts \parencite{tobin2017}: distributing training capacity across a range of conditions costs accuracy at any single condition but buys insensitivity across the range. The practical consequence is a design decision rather than a universally correct answer. Where the deployment noise level is known and stable---a calibrated laboratory instrument---fixed-noise training at the measured level is preferable. Where it is uncertain, drifting, or varies between devices, randomised training sacrifices approximately 6\,pp at nominal conditions to avoid a 15\,pp loss at elevated ones. Mixed training additionally retains clean-domain competence (70.40\% at 0$\times$ against 41.44\% for online augmentation), making it the most defensible default when the deployment environment is uncharacterised.

The 0$\times$ column is informative in itself. All three regimes lose accuracy on perfectly clean data, most severely online augmentation. As established in Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation-minimal}, this is largely the no-contact class: a model trained only on corrupted measurements has never observed a true zero vector.

All five comparisons remain significant after correction. The effect sizes are extreme by the conventions of applied statistics and should be interpreted with care: they arise because the conditions are almost completely separated with very small fold-to-fold variance, which is expected when a deterministic pipeline is applied to disjoint partitions of a synthetic dataset. They establish that the differences are systematic rather than sampling artefacts; they do not imply corresponding practical margins on hardware.

The cross-validated estimates are also slightly more stable than the repeated-split estimates in Table~\ref{tab:main-results-raw}---most visibly for Clean$\to$Clean (96.26 $\pm$ 1.89 against 94.00 $\pm$ 6.89). The two protocols differ in what they vary: the 5-fold procedure varies the partition while holding initialisation fixed, whereas the multi-seed protocol varies both. The larger multi-seed spread is therefore the more conservative estimate of the CNN's clean-domain reliability, and is the one quoted in the headline table.

\subsection{Per-Class Vulnerability}
\label{sec:res-perclass}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{results/figures/per_class_robustness.png}
\caption{Per-class accuracy against noise severity for the noise-augmented CNN. Point contact is the weakest class throughout; the no-contact class is undetectable at 0$\times$ severity because it appears as an exact zero vector never encountered in training.}
\label{fig:per-class}
\end{figure}

Per-class behaviour (Figure~\ref{fig:per-class}) is not uniform, and the ordering is physically interpretable when read against the signal magnitudes of Table~\ref{tab:noise-decomposition}. \emph{Point contact} is the weakest class at every severity (39\% at 0.5$\times$ falling to 29\% at 3$\times$), consistent with two compounding factors: it has the second-smallest voltage signature ($L_2 = 0.0058$) and, as established by the mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}), it is intrinsically sub-mesh-scale, making it the class whose simulation is least faithful. Its poor classification should therefore be attributed partly to the model and partly to the fidelity limits of its representation.

\emph{Firm press} and \emph{distributed contact}, the two largest-amplitude classes, are the most reliably recovered among the contact classes. \emph{Light touch} improves slightly with severity (75.7\% to 90.9\%), which appears counter-intuitive but follows from the same mechanism as the Gaussian result above: additional noise makes the smallest-signal classes distinguishable from the no-contact class rather than being absorbed into it. Accuracy for \emph{no contact} is 0\% at 0$\times$ and $\geq$97\% at every non-zero severity, again reflecting that a noise-trained model does not recognise a noiseless zero vector.

The safety implication is specific. The transition most relevant to prosthetic grasp control---detecting the onset of contact---is reliable under the noise conditions the model was trained for, but the class carrying the highest local pressure (point contact) is the least reliable, and its confusion is with other contact classes rather than with no-contact. A controller could therefore be expected to detect \emph{that} contact has occurred more reliably than to characterise \emph{what kind}.

\label{sec:res-gaussian}

Accuracy varies by less than 0.3\,pp across a 40\,dB range---four orders of magnitude in noise power---for every model tested. A clean-trained classifier that collapses to 19.98\% under the full noise model (Table~\ref{tab:main-results-raw}) is essentially unaffected by Gaussian corruption even at 20\,dB. Additive zero-mean noise is largely absorbed by batch normalisation \parencite{ioffe2015batchnorm} and does not disturb the geometry separating the classes.

This bears directly on how the accuracies reported in the reviewed literature should be read. Robustness demonstrated under Gaussian-only augmentation \parencite{chen2023, park2022skin} is not evidence of robustness to the distortions that dominate real measurements; a model can be entirely robust to Gaussian noise and still fail completely on hardware. The ablation of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation} makes the same point constructively: Gaussian noise is necessary in a training noise model, but for the narrow reason that it establishes the measurement noise floor, not because it represents the principal challenge.

\label{sec:res-calibration}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

\label{tab:calibration}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:calibration} reports Expected Calibration Error across conditions. The clean-trained model is well calibrated in its own domain (ECE~=~0.025) and catastrophically miscalibrated outside it (ECE~=~0.800), reporting 99.7\% mean confidence while achieving 19.7\% accuracy. This is the most dangerous failure mode available to a safety-critical classifier: the softmax output carries no usable warning that the model has left its domain, so neither a user nor an automatic grasp controller could detect the failure from the model's own outputs.

The noise-augmented model is by contrast well calibrated on noisy data (ECE~=~0.047), which refines the general finding of \textcite{guo2017calibration} that modern networks are overconfident by construction. Overconfidence here is a symptom of \emph{domain shift} rather than an intrinsic property of the architecture: when the training distribution matches deployment, calibration is recovered without any post-hoc correction. Its residual miscalibration on clean data (ECE~=~0.172, now in the direction of \emph{under}-confidence at 55.4\% mean confidence) is the same distributional artefact seen throughout.

The practical recommendation is nonetheless unchanged, because the deployment distribution is never known exactly: confidence estimates from a simulation-trained EIT classifier should not be treated as calibrated until validated on target hardware, and temperature scaling or an explicit uncertainty estimate should be fitted on target-domain data before any deployment claim is made.

\subsection{Characterising the Domain Gap}
\label{sec:res-domain}

The dominance of electrode bias established by ablation can be verified directly from the data, independently of classifier performance. Decomposing the measurement vectors into signal and noise contributions (Table~\ref{tab:noise-decomposition}) shows how extreme the imbalance is.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Signal and noise energy decomposition by class ($L_2$ norms, means over the dataset). Electrode bias accounts for essentially all noise energy and exceeds the touch signal itself by an order of magnitude or more.}
\label{tab:noise-decomposition}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Signal} & \textbf{Total noise} & \textbf{Bias} & \textbf{Signal:residual} \\
\midrule
No contact          & 0.0000 & 0.1669 & 0.1656 & --- \\
Light touch         & 0.0040 & 0.1655 & 0.1655 & 11.3 \\
Firm press          & 0.0248 & 0.1657 & 0.1657 & 11.5 \\
Point contact       & 0.0058 & 0.1657 & 0.1657 & 11.3 \\
Distributed contact & 0.0359 & 0.1654 & 0.1653 & 11.9 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Electrode bias accounts for effectively 100\% of total noise energy in every class. More strikingly, it exceeds the touch signal itself by a wide margin: for light touch the bias norm (0.1655) is approximately \textbf{41 times} the signal norm (0.0040), and even for the largest class (distributed contact, 0.0359) it remains 4.6 times larger. In the raw measurement, the touch signature is a small perturbation on a much larger structured offset (Figures~\ref{fig:app-noise-decomposition}--\ref{fig:app-signal-noise}, Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

Classification is nevertheless feasible because the bias is \emph{structured} rather than random: it is constant within each electrode's measurement block and varies only between electrodes. Once removed, the signal-to-residual ratio is a comfortable 11.3--11.9 across all contact classes. The learning problem is therefore not extracting a signal from overwhelming random noise, but learning to discount a large, low-dimensional, block-structured offset---precisely the structure a convolutional kernel spanning adjacent measurements can represent, which explains the CNN's 17.9\,pp advantage over the exchangeable baselines in Table~\ref{tab:main-results-raw}.

This decomposition independently corroborates the ablation. Electrode bias must be modelled because it \emph{is} the domain gap in energy terms; Gaussian noise is required for the separate reason established in Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation-minimal}; and contact impedance and quantisation are negligible in both analyses. Three lines of evidence---component ablation, severity response and direct energy decomposition---converge on the same conclusion.

The conclusions are insensitive to SNR and to contact impedance across their full plausible ranges (variation $<$0.5\,pp), which is reassuring given that contact impedance is the least well constrained parameter (Section~\ref{sec:method-noise-components}). Two parameters do matter. Accuracy collapses to 35.0\% when electrode bias is quadrupled to $b_{\max} = 0.08$, and to 30.1\% at 8-bit ADC resolution. Both are interpretable: the former pushes the structured offset far beyond the amplitude at which the model learned to discount it, while the latter raises the quantisation step until it becomes comparable to the touch signal itself, which for light touch is only 0.0040.

These thresholds are the most directly actionable results for hardware design, because they are expressed in quantities an engineer specifies. Electrode-placement tolerance and ADC bit depth both require explicit budgets; SNR and electrode--substrate impedance uniformity, which typically receive the most attention, can be relaxed considerably without measurable cost. Hyperparameter sensitivity is correspondingly modest---accuracy varies by 2.2--5.4\,pp across learning rate, dropout, weight decay and training severity range (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity})---confirming that the findings are not an artefact of the specific training configuration.

\subsection{Generalisation Across Noise Realisations}
\label{sec:res-memorisation}

Because train and test noise are drawn from the same parametric family, it must be established that the model accommodates the noise \emph{distribution} rather than memorising the particular realisations in its training set. Two experiments test this.

\paragraph{Independent noise draws.} The trained noisy-domain CNN was evaluated on ten independently generated noisy test sets, each with entirely different electrode-bias vectors, contact-impedance factors and Gaussian realisations. Accuracy was 75.61\% $\pm$ 0.17\% across the ten draws, against 74.37\% on the original MATLAB-generated test set. The variation across realisations (0.17\,pp) is far smaller than any effect discussed in this chapter, which is direct evidence of distributional accommodation rather than memorisation. That the alternative draws score marginally \emph{higher} than the original reflects the small implementation difference between the MATLAB and Python noise paths, and bounds that difference at approximately 1.2\,pp.

\paragraph{Persistent per-sample noise.} A CNN was trained with noise sampled once per training sample and held fixed across all epochs, mimicking a population of devices each with its own persistent electrode characteristics. It achieved 69.09\% on noisy evaluation and 69.47\% on an independent noise draw---statistically indistinguishable from online augmentation (69.50\%, Table~\ref{tab:augmented-results}). Whether the training noise persists per sample or is redrawn each epoch therefore has no measurable effect on deployed accuracy, provided training and deployment distributions overlap. What matters is distributional coverage, not realisation-level correspondence: a considerably less restrictive requirement for practical sim-to-real transfer than device-specific calibration would be.
