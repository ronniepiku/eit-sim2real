\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter critically interprets the experimental findings in the context of the research objectives (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), prior literature (Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}), and deployment implications. The discussion is structured around five themes: the nature of the sim-to-real gap (\S\ref{sec:disc-gap}), a critical examination of approximation error and the inverse crime (\S\ref{sec:disc-approx-error}), the augmentation paradox (\S\ref{sec:disc-paradox}), class-level vulnerability analysis (\S\ref{sec:disc-class}), and deployment readiness (\S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}).

\section{Nature of the Simulation-to-Reality Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

The universal collapse to chance level under Clean$\to$Noisy evaluation ($\approx$20\% across all architectures and feature spaces) provides the clearest evidence yet that the sim-to-real gap in \gls{eit} tactile sensing is not addressable through model capacity alone. This finding extends the corruption robustness results of \textcite{hendrycks2019}---who demonstrated up to 40\% degradation for image classifiers---to a more extreme regime where multi-component noise completely eliminates learned representations. The statistical confirmation ($p < 0.0001$, Cohen's $d = -56.7$) establishes this beyond reasonable doubt. The exceptionally large effect size reflects the complete distributional mismatch between clean and noisy feature spaces---a more extreme regime than the partial degradations typically studied in domain adaptation, where test-time data retains some overlap with the training distribution.

Critically, the domain gap analysis (MMD = 0.384) localises the problem to a single dominant component: electrode positioning bias contributes 99.8\% of total noise energy and dominates the MMD (0.387 of 0.384 total). This structural insight reframes the sim-to-real challenge: it is not fundamentally a random-noise problem but a \emph{systematic bias problem}. Each electrode acquires a fixed but unknown offset that is constant within its 13 measurements but varies between electrodes, producing block-structured corruption in the 208-D vector. This connects directly to the electrode modelling concerns raised by \textcite{vilhunen2002} and \textcite{kolehmainen1997}, who documented similar structured artefacts in reconstruction contexts but did not evaluate their impact on downstream classification tasks.

The CNN's superiority on noisy data (+17.8pp over the next-best model, RF at 57.9\%) can be attributed to its convolutional architecture. Kernels of size 5 spanning adjacent measurements naturally learn to detect and compensate for the block-structured bias pattern, functioning as implicit per-electrode bias-subtraction filters. This architectural advantage is absent in fully-connected models (MLP) and kernel methods (SVM) which treat each of the 208 measurements independently without exploiting their cyclic structure. The finding supports the argument of \textcite{lecun2015} that inductive biases matching the data structure are essential for robust generalisation, and extends this principle from spatial images to 1D measurement sequences with periodic structure.

The Gaussian-only comparison provides perhaps the most important contextualisation for the broader \gls{eit} community. Under the noise model assumed by \textcite{yao2022} and \textcite{chen2023}, the clean-trained CNN achieves $>$99\% accuracy at every SNR level down to 20\,dB. This means prior reported accuracies of 98--99\% under Gaussian noise \parencite{yao2022} do not demonstrate meaningful robustness, they merely confirm that additive Gaussian noise at typical SNR levels is trivially handled. Only the multi-component model exposes genuine vulnerability, motivating this work's contribution.

The ablation results additionally support the supervisor's distinction between component-wise and simultaneous training. Within the controlled multi-seed ablation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation}), the full 4-component model remains the diagnostic reference condition, while matched component-specific runs reveal how much robustness each isolated corruption can sustain on its own. This supports the \emph{foundational mode} interpretation: robust deployment requires training data that jointly represents all dominant error sources, with component-specific training used primarily to diagnose failure mechanisms and interaction structure rather than as a standalone robustness strategy.

\section{Approximation Error and the Inverse Crime}
\label{sec:disc-approx-error}

An important methodological consideration for any simulation-trained classifier is the relationship between the \emph{inverse crime} \parencite{wirgin2004inverse, kaipio2007statistical} and the broader \emph{approximation error} framework \parencite{kaipio2005statistical}. In the classical inverse-problem literature, the inverse crime occurs when the same numerical discretisation is used to both generate synthetic data and solve the inverse problem, producing unrealistically favourable results because discretisation errors cancel exactly. \textcite{kaipio2007statistical} formalised this within a Bayesian framework, demonstrating that unaccounted modelling errors (mesh discretisation, dimensional reduction, material simplification) constitute a structured approximation error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ that must be statistically characterised to avoid systematic bias in the solution:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\text{measured}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}} \approx \mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}
\label{eq:approx-error}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}$ denote the true and simplified forward operators respectively, and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}(\sigma) - \mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}(\sigma)$ captures the systematic discrepancy. \textcite{nissinen2009compensation} demonstrated experimentally that compensating for this approximation error---by augmenting the noise model with a statistical characterisation of $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$---substantially improves \gls{eit} reconstruction quality on real hardware.

\paragraph{Applicability to classification.} The standard approximation error framework is designed for \emph{inverse problems}, augmenting a Bayesian likelihood function with the statistics of the modelling discrepancy. The present work does not solve an inverse problem---it classifies voltage vectors directly, bypassing reconstruction entirely. There is no likelihood function to augment, and no iterative solver whose convergence depends on model fidelity. This fundamental distinction substantially reduces the severity of the inverse crime concern: the classifier operates on the \emph{output} of the forward model (boundary voltages) rather than using the forward model \emph{within} an inversion loop, meaning there is no opportunity for numerical cancellation between generation and solution discretisations.

Nevertheless, the \emph{principle} of approximation error remains relevant in a more subtle form. The forward model introduces systematic biases through several simplifications (Table~\ref{tab:approx-errors}), and a classifier trained exclusively on data from one model instance could learn to exploit artefacts of that specific discretisation rather than physically meaningful features.

\paragraph{Noise augmentation as implicit approximation error compensation.} A novel interpretation emerges when viewing the noise model through the lens of \textcite{kaipio2005statistical}: the four-component noise augmentation implicitly compensates for a subset of the forward model's approximation errors. Specifically, the contact impedance component ($\sigma_z = 10\%$, Equation~\ref{eq:noise-impedance}) models precisely the electrode--substrate interface discrepancy that the CEM idealises; the electrode bias component ($b_{\max} = 0.02$, Equation~\ref{eq:noise-bias}) captures the structured measurement offsets that arise from geometric simplification of electrode placement. These are exactly the categories of approximation error that \textcite{nissinen2009compensation} demonstrated require explicit compensation in reconstruction contexts. Where the Bayesian approach characterises $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ statistically and incorporates it into the likelihood, noise-augmented classification achieves an analogous effect through training-time distribution enrichment: exposing the classifier to the \emph{range} of possible approximation-error-induced perturbations, forcing it to learn representations invariant to these structured biases.

This connection has a significant implication. The observation that electrode bias accounts for 99.8\% of the domain gap (Section~\ref{sec:disc-gap}) can be reinterpreted as evidence that the dominant approximation error is not mesh discretisation or dimensional reduction, but rather the idealisation of the \emph{electrode--substrate interface}---consistent with \textcite{vilhunen2002}'s finding that contact impedance modelling errors dominate reconstruction artefacts in real \gls{eit} systems. The noise model's electrode-level components therefore address the \emph{most critical} approximation error, even though they were originally motivated by measurement noise rather than forward-model discrepancy.

\paragraph{Mesh-level inverse crime.} The mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}, Table~\ref{tab:mesh-convergence}) shows that mesh-level approximation error is class-dependent. For the four spatially-extended classes (light touch, firm press, distributed contact) the mean relative voltage difference between the medium and fine discretisations is 8--18\,\%---of comparable order of magnitude to, but smaller than, the dominant electrode-bias component (which contributes 99.8\,\% of total noise energy in the multi-component model). For the point-contact class the mesh-error figure (77\,\%) is not a meaningful convergence statistic because the contact radius is intrinsically sub-mesh-scale at all tractable refinements; the discretisation is therefore better characterised as a deliberate representational choice (single-element perturbation via the nearest-element fallback in \texttt{generate\_sample.m}) than as a numerical artefact. The stronger evidence against a mesh-level inverse crime is functional: the CNN's robustness to noise at 3$\times$ training severity (93.3\,\% accuracy) demonstrates that the learned features are insensitive to perturbations far exceeding any plausible discretisation artefact. Were the classifier exploiting mesh-specific numerical structure, it would degrade under noise that disrupts such structure---the opposite of what is observed. This constitutes a functional, classification-specific test of mesh independence that complements the traditional dual-mesh approach used in inverse-problem settings \parencite{kaipio2007statistical}.

\paragraph{Residual uncompensated errors.} The 2D$\to$3D geometric reduction remains the largest uncharacterised approximation error. Out-of-plane current flow in a real 3D sensor produces voltage patterns with no analogue in the 2D model---a systematic, structured discrepancy that cannot be compensated through random noise augmentation because it is deterministic and geometry-dependent. This represents a genuine residual inverse crime that future work must address through either 3D forward modelling or empirical calibration against physical hardware. Critically, this is a \emph{model fidelity} limitation rather than a \emph{noise model} limitation---the domain randomisation framework can only compensate for errors that manifest as stochastic perturbations to the measurement vector, not deterministic geometric biases.

\section{The Augmentation Paradox}
\label{sec:disc-paradox}

The most counter-intuitive finding is the complete failure of online noise augmentation (19.4\%) despite the success of fixed noise training (75.7\%). This contradicts the established domain randomisation literature \parencite{tobin2017}, where diverse augmentation during training typically improves target-domain generalisation, and more broadly conflicts with the data augmentation best practices surveyed by \textcite{shorten2019}. The explanation lies in the temporal structure of the dominant noise component and exposes a previously unidentified failure mode of domain randomisation.

Electrode bias is \emph{fixed per sample} in both the training dataset and test dataset (drawn once during data generation). When the CNN is trained on the pre-generated noisy dataset, it encounters \emph{consistent} bias patterns across epochs for each sample. Over training, it learns to associate specific bias patterns with their true labels---effectively learning a joint model of (signal + fixed bias) $\to$ label. At test time, the test set shares the same noise generation process, so the learned bias-compensation transfers. However, the different-noise-draw test (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}) confirms that this reflects accommodation of the bias \emph{distribution} rather than memorisation of specific bias vectors: the model retains its accuracy when evaluated on independently-generated noise realisations it never encountered during training.

In contrast, online augmentation draws \emph{new random bias} at each epoch. The same clean sample receives different bias patterns across epochs, so the only \emph{consistent} information is the underlying clean signal. The model converges to a clean-signal extractor that achieves 98.6\% on clean data but cannot accommodate the persistent bias encountered at test time. Formally, if $\mathbf{x}_{\text{noisy}} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}} + \mathbf{b}$ where $\mathbf{b}$ is fixed at test time but randomised during training, the model learns $f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}} + \mathbf{b}]) = f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}})$ because $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}] = \mathbf{0}$ by the zero-mean bias assumption.

This finding has significant theoretical implications. Domain randomisation \parencite{tobin2017} succeeds when the test-time domain is a \emph{sample} from the randomisation distribution---i.e., when noise at deployment is also random and i.i.d.\ per frame. But real \gls{eit} hardware has \emph{fixed} electrode characteristics that produce consistent per-session bias, violating this assumption. The fixed-bias augmentation experiment (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}) confirms this analysis: sampling noise once per training sample and holding it fixed across epochs (mimicking per-device persistence) \emph{recovers} strong noisy-domain accuracy (69.5\%), even though the training noise is an independent realisation from the test set. This demonstrates that the critical factor is per-sample noise \emph{persistence} across epochs rather than exact distributional match: any epoch-invariant bias provides a stable target that the model can accommodate, whereas per-epoch re-randomisation collapses the expected bias to zero and forces a clean-signal extractor. The appropriate augmentation strategy must therefore preserve per-device noise persistence---sampling a fixed per-device noise realisation that persists across training epochs, drawn from a distribution overlapping the target hardware's characteristics---rather than re-randomising noise every frame. Where the target distribution is poorly characterised, explicit domain adaptation \parencite{ganin2016, tzeng2017adda} using a small amount of calibration data from the target device offers a complementary route. This insight represents a refinement of the domain randomisation framework specific to sensor modalities with fixed per-device characteristics---a category that extends beyond \gls{eit} to strain gauges, capacitive arrays, and other transducer systems exhibiting manufacturing variation.

\section{Per-Class Vulnerability and Physical Interpretation}
\label{sec:disc-class}

The per-class robustness profiling reveals a physically interpretable vulnerability hierarchy. Light touch ($-$18.5pp at 3$\times$ severity) is most affected because its signal magnitude (L2 = 0.004) is comparable to---indeed slightly below---the mean residual noise floor (L2 = 0.005 after bias removal). As noise severity increases, the signal-to-noise ratio falls below unity and the class boundary erodes. This connects to the detection threshold analysis of \textcite{boone1996}, who established a minimum conductivity contrast of $\sim$2\% for reliable EIT detection---light touch operates near this boundary by design (5--15\% $\Delta\sigma$).

The t-SNE visualisations confirm that light touch and point contact clusters overlap under noise, as do firm press and distributed contact. This pairing is physically motivated: classes with similar L2 norms but different spatial extent become indistinguishable when spatial information is corrupted. The model conflates \emph{magnitude} with \emph{extent}---a physically reasonable confusion given that both produce similar integral voltage perturbations, differing primarily in the spatial distribution of those perturbations across electrode pairs.

The Noisy$\to$Clean asymmetry (52.0\%) reveals a complementary failure mode: the noisy-trained CNN never encounters the zero-vector class 0 (no contact) without additive bias, and systematically misclassifies clean zero-vectors as light touch. This is not a model deficiency but a training distribution problem---addressable through mixed training with correct bias persistence, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-paradox}.

\section{Deployment Readiness and Safety Implications}
\label{sec:disc-deployment}

Three findings have direct implications for prosthetic deployment:

\paragraph{Calibration failure.} The catastrophic overconfidence (ECE = 0.796) under domain shift means that raw softmax outputs cannot serve as uncertainty indicators in safety-critical applications \parencite{guo2017calibration}. A prosthetic skin reporting 99.6\% confidence while achieving 20\% accuracy would provide dangerously misleading feedback to both the user and any autonomous grasp controller. In medical device contexts, this constitutes a systematic hazard under IEC 62304 software lifecycle standards, where predictive confidence must be validated against operational accuracy. Post-hoc calibration methods (temperature scaling) or explicit uncertainty quantification (Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles) are prerequisite for deployment---an aspect entirely absent from prior \gls{eit} classification studies \parencite{yao2022, chen2023}. The ECE improvement from 0.796 to 0.051 when training and test distributions match further suggests that calibration failure is a \emph{domain shift phenomenon} rather than an architectural deficiency.

\paragraph{Noise model completeness.} The sensitivity analysis confirms that results generalise across physically plausible parameter ranges (accuracy $>$97\% for all parameters except extreme 20\,dB SNR). This suggests the noise model captures the dominant degradation modes robustly. However, temporal drift remains unaddressed. \textcite{lee2019} documented progressive baseline shifts in hydrogel substrates over 24-hour periods, a sequential phenomenon requiring temporal models rather than per-frame augmentation.

This conclusion should be read alongside recent hardware advances. \textcite{dong2025lattice} show that silicone-encapsulated lattice hydrogels improve short-horizon mechanical durability and sensitivity compared with hydrogel-only layers, directly addressing fragility concerns in repeated use. Yet their durability evidence is still limited to short cycle counts (approximately 100 cycles), and their robustness analysis remains Gaussian-centric. The two lines of work are therefore complementary rather than competing: morphology optimisation can improve signal quality and survivability, while foundational multi-error training addresses residual measurement uncertainty under realistic deployment conditions.

\paragraph{Component prioritisation for hardware design.} The ablation identifies contact impedance as the fastest-degrading component under severity scaling ($-$2.6pp from 0$\times$ to 3$\times$), suggesting that electrode-substrate interface quality is the highest-priority hardware improvement. Quantisation (16-bit) is effectively invisible, confirming that standard ADC resolution is more than sufficient for \gls{eit} tactile sensing. This empirical prioritisation---electrode interface $>$ noise floor $>$ measurement chain $>$ digitisation---provides actionable guidance for hardware designers, absent from prior computational studies. The finding that contact impedance degrades performance multiplicatively (consistent with its effect on drive current magnitude) while Gaussian noise is merely additive further clarifies why the multi-component model is essential: the dominant degradation mechanism is non-additive and therefore cannot be characterised by SNR alone.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Several limitations bound the generalisability of these findings and must be acknowledged transparently:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Matched noise distribution.} The 75.7\% Noisy$\to$Noisy result uses the same noise generation process for train and test data, raising the question of whether the CNN has learned genuine noise-invariant features or has merely memorised the statistical structure of the specific noise distribution. Since train and test noise are drawn from the same parametric family (Gaussian biases with known $\sigma$), the model may exploit distributional regularities that would not transfer to a different physical system. This result therefore represents an \emph{upper bound} for matched-distribution deployment; genuine sim-to-real transfer with unknown real noise would likely yield lower accuracy.
    
    Three observations provide empirical evidence against pure memorisation. First, the different noise draw test (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}) directly evaluates the trained model on independently-generated noisy test sets with entirely different random realisations---producing different electrode bias vectors, contact impedance factors, and Gaussian noise instances---and finds that accuracy remains highly stable (75.8\% $\pm$ 0.2\%). This demonstrates that the model generalises across noise \emph{instances} rather than memorising specific bias patterns. Second, the 5-fold cross-validation protocol evaluates on held-out test samples whose specific noise realisations were never seen during training, yet performance remains consistent (std = 0.7\%). Third, the severity sweep demonstrates graceful degradation at noise levels \emph{exceeding} the training distribution (93.3\% at 3$\times$ severity), which would not occur if the model had merely overfit to the training-time noise statistics. Nevertheless, a definitive test---training on one noise distribution and evaluating on a distinct noise distribution with different \emph{parametric} statistics (e.g., different $\sigma$ for electrode bias)---remains necessary to fully characterise the model's transfer limits and is identified as important future work.
    
    \item \textbf{2D geometry.} The forward model uses a 2D cross-section; 3D geometries introduce additional sensitivity patterns, out-of-plane current flow, and electrode coupling effects not captured here \parencite{adler2006}. Extension to 3D would increase the feature space substantially ($>$1000 measurements) and may alter the relative impact of noise components.
    
    \item \textbf{Sub-mesh-scale point contact.} The mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}, Table~\ref{tab:mesh-convergence}) established that the point-contact class (radius 0.02--0.05 on a unit-circle mesh) is intrinsically a sub-mesh-scale perturbation: even at the fine refinement, only 2.5 elements on average intersect the contact region, and 4 of 20 samples are under-resolved ($\le 1$ element intersected). The data-generation pipeline therefore represents point contact as a single-element conductivity perturbation via a nearest-element fallback, in the spirit of a discrete localised load \parencite{kim2019}, rather than a fully resolved disk. Real point contacts on hardware would produce localised pressure spikes that no FEM at this geometry could fully resolve; bridging this gap requires either a 3D forward model with substantially refined local meshing near the contact, or empirical calibration against physical hardware. This is identified as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}. The implication for the present findings is that the point-contact class should be interpreted as a \emph{representative} of high-pressure focal contacts rather than a quantitatively faithful simulation of one; the noise-robustness conclusions for this class therefore characterise the classifier's behaviour on a stylised single-element perturbation, and quantitative transfer to real point-contact stimuli will require model-fidelity improvements orthogonal to the noise augmentation framework developed here.
    
    \item \textbf{Static single-frame classification.} Temporal correlations in sequential measurements, which could aid classification through pattern-over-time features, are not exploited \parencite{silvera-tawil2012eit}. Recurrent or attention-based architectures processing measurement sequences may achieve superior noise robustness by leveraging temporal redundancy.
    
    \item \textbf{No hardware validation.} All results are simulation-based; the noise model is literature-justified but not empirically calibrated against a specific physical system. The assumed Gaussian distribution for electrode bias is a modelling choice---real bias distributions may be multimodal or heavy-tailed.
    
    \item \textbf{Ablation protocol mismatch with main grid.} Although the ablation is now aggregated across five seeds and the Python-side noise path is physics-consistent, it still uses a dedicated protocol and condition set optimised for component analysis rather than the full main-grid evaluation matrix. Consequently, ablation values should be interpreted primarily for relative component ranking and mechanism diagnosis, not as direct substitutes for the main-condition performance figures.

    \item \textbf{Exploratory memorisation diagnostics.} The different-draw and fixed-bias analyses are currently exploratory and not yet repeated under the same full multi-seed protocol used for the primary condition matrix. Their qualitative conclusions are informative for mechanism, but the quantitative effect sizes should be treated as provisional until multi-seed replication is completed.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations are inherent to the simulation-first research design chosen to isolate the noise augmentation effect under controlled conditions. They do not invalidate the core findings---which demonstrate the \emph{principle} of noise-matched training and identify electrode bias as the dominant gap component---but constrain the quantitative confidence with which specific accuracy figures can be projected onto real hardware deployment.
