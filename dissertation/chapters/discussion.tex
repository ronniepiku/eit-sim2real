\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the findings of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} against the research objectives, the prior literature, and the requirements of deployment. It is organised around five themes: the structure of the simulation-to-reality gap (\S\ref{sec:disc-gap}), what the ablation implies for noise-model design (\S\ref{sec:disc-ablation}), the trade-off governing choice of training regime (\S\ref{sec:disc-tradeoff}), the validity of a simulation-only study (\S\ref{sec:disc-approx-error}), and deployment readiness (\S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}). Limitations are stated in \S\ref{sec:disc-limitations}.

\section{The Structure of the Sim-2-Real Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

The most consequential finding of this work is that the simulation-to-reality gap in \gls{eit} tactile classification is dominated by a single, highly structured error source, and that this changes what an effective response looks like.

The universal collapse to chance under Clean$\to$Noisy evaluation ($\approx$20\% across four architectures and three feature representations) shows the gap is not a capacity problem. Adding parameters, changing inductive bias, or reducing dimensionality all fail, because none addresses the cause. The training distribution does not contain the deployment distribution. This extends the corruption-robustness results of \textcite{hendrycks2019} to a considerably more extreme regime, where image classifiers degrade by up to 40\% and essentially lose all discriminative ability, and is consistent with the bound of \textcite{ben-david2010domain}, in which target error is limited by source error plus distributional divergence.

Electrode positioning bias accounts for effectively 100\% of noise energy, and for the light-touch class it exceeds the touch signal itself by a factor of roughly 41. A clean-trained classifier is therefore not being asked to tolerate a perturbed version of a familiar input; it is presented with a vector whose dominant component it has never seen, in which the informative part is a small residual. Viewed this way, chance-level performance is the expected outcome.

Crucially, that dominant component is \emph{structured}, constant within each electrode's measurement block, varying only between electrodes, and therefore confined to a low-dimensional subspace of the 208-dimensional measurement. This is what makes the problem tractable at all. After the bias is discounted, the signal-to-residual ratio is a comfortable 11.3--11.9. It also explains the architectural result. The CNN's 17.9\, pp advantage over the strongest exchangeable baseline is not a general claim about deep learning; it follows from convolutional kernels spanning adjacent measurements being able to represent a block-constant offset compactly, whereas an \gls{mlp} or \gls{svm} treating the measurements exchangeably must learn that structure from data with no architectural prior for it. Where a distortion has known structure, the informative design decision is to match the inductive bias to it, which supports the argument of \textcite{lecun2015} in a setting far removed from natural images.

This structural view also reframes the failure of dimensionality reduction (Appendix~\ref{app:feature-reduction}). \gls{pca} and \gls{lda} fitted on clean data discard precisely the directions along which the bias acts, because those directions carry no clean-domain class information. The reduction is optimal for the source domain and pathological for the target, a concrete instance of a principle that is easy to state abstractly and easy to violate in practice.

\section{What the Ablation Implies for Noise-Model Design}
\label{sec:disc-ablation}

The ablation (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation}) yields the study's most directly transferable result, and it depends entirely on a methodological choice the prior literature does not make.

\paragraph{Matched evaluation misleads.} Evaluating a component-ablated model on data containing only that component measures the difficulty of an artificial world, not the value of modelling the component. The two evaluations disagree completely: matched evaluation ranks electrode bias as the most damaging component and Gaussian noise as benign, while deployment evaluation reverses this, showing Gaussian-only, contact-impedance-only and quantisation-only models to be worthless in the field (14.03--20.88\%, at or below chance) whereas the electrode-bias model retains 57.79\%. Had only the matched column been reported (the convention in the studies reviewed in Section~\ref{sec: lit-ml-concerns}), the guidance derived from it would have been exactly inverted. Since real hardware always exhibits all error sources simultaneously, deployment evaluation is the only operationally meaningful protocol, and this correction applies to component ablations generally, not only to \gls{eit}.

\paragraph{A two-component model suffices.} Gaussian noise combined with electrode bias reaches 76.53\%, level with the best three-component subset (76.90\%), the full four-component model (76.62\%) and the headline result obtained on the pre-generated noisy dataset (75.81\%). The two are required for different reasons. Electrode bias because it constitutes the domain shift, and Gaussian noise because it establishes the measurement noise floor, without which the no-contact class is an exact zero vector in training but a noisy vector at test time---a mechanism isolated by the 20.1\, pp step in the per-component severity sweep, corresponding almost exactly to the 20\% of samples in that class.

This is a useful negative result as well as a positive one. Contact impedance and quantisation, both physically real and carefully parametrised from the experimental literature, contribute nothing measurable at 16-bit resolution. A practitioner can therefore halve the noise model without cost, provided the two retained components are the correct ones. The corollary matters more, as omitting electrode bias---the component prior work omits---reduces the model to chance regardless of what else is included.

\paragraph{Ordering is not free.} The 21.6\,pp difference between Gaussian~$\to$~electrode bias (76.53\%) and electrode bias~$\to$~Gaussian (54.98\%) for the same two components is, on inspection, a parametrisation artefact rather than a physical effect. The Gaussian component is specified relative to signal amplitude, so applying it after a bias roughly forty times larger than the signal inflates its magnitude accordingly. It is nonetheless a real trap for compositional noise models, and one invisible unless orderings are enumerated. Any component defined relative to the running signal must be applied at its physically correct point in the measurement chain.

\section{Choosing a Training Regime}
\label{sec:disc-tradeoff}

The severity sweep (Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}) shows that no training regime dominates, and that the appropriate choice depends on how well the deployment environment is characterised.

Fixed-noise training achieves the highest accuracy at the severity it was trained on (75.95\% at 1$\times$) but degrades monotonically beyond it, losing 15.0\, pp by 3$\times$. Randomised regimes trained over severities sampled from $[0.5, 2.0]$ are markedly flatter---online augmentation varies by only 1.4\, pp between 1$\times$ and 3$\times$---and overtake fixed-noise training at approximately 2.2$\times$. This is the behaviour domain randomisation predicts \parencite{tobin2017}, where spreading training capacity across conditions costs accuracy at any one condition and buys insensitivity across the range.

The resulting guidance is conditional rather than universal. Where the deployment noise level is known and stable, fixed-noise training at the measured level is preferable, and the randomisation penalty ($\approx$6\, pp) is unjustified. Where it is uncertain, drifts over time, or varies between manufactured devices, which is the realistic case for a prosthetic worn daily, randomisation trades that 6\, pp at nominal conditions against a 15\, pp loss at elevated ones, and the trade is worth making. Mixed training is the most defensible default when the environment is uncharacterised. It retains 70.40\% clean-domain accuracy, compared to 41.44\% with online augmentation, while matching it on noisy data.

An important secondary result is that \emph{how} the noise is presented during training does not matter, provided the distribution is right. Training with noise resampled every epoch and training with noise fixed once per sample yield deployed accuracies that differ by less than the seed-to-seed spread (69.50\% and 69.09\%). Combined with stability across ten independent noise realisations (75.61\% $\pm$ 0.17\%), this establishes that the model learns distributional accommodation rather than memorising particular realisations. The implication is encouraging for sim-to-real transfer, because what is required is that the training distribution \emph{covers} the deployment distribution, not that it reproduce a specific device's noise signature. Per-device calibration, which would be a serious obstacle for a consumer prosthetic, is not a prerequisite.

The clean-domain cost of noise-aware training deserves emphasis because it is easy to overlook. Every noise-trained regime loses accuracy on uncorrupted data, and the loss is concentrated almost entirely in the no-contact class, which a noise-trained model does not recognise as a noiseless zero vector. For a device that may be operated immediately after calibration, this failure mode is safety-relevant, and mixed training is the straightforward mitigation.

\section{Validity of a Simulation-Only Study}
\label{sec:disc-approx-error}

Any classifier trained purely on synthetic data must address whether its conclusions survive contact with real measurements. The relevant framework is the \emph{approximation error} formulation of \textcite{kaipio2005statistical}, in which the accurate and simplified forward operators are related by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\text{measured}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}} \approx \mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}},
\label{eq:approx-error}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ is the systematic discrepancy introduced by modelling simplifications. \textcite{nissinen2009compensation} showed experimentally that characterising this term statistically and folding it into the noise model substantially improves \gls{eit} reconstruction on real hardware.

\paragraph{The inverse crime is attenuated but not absent.} The classical inverse crime \parencite{wirgin2004inverse, kaipio2007statistical} arises when the same discretisation generates the data and solves the inverse problem, so that discretisation errors cancel. The present work does not solve an inverse problem: it classifies boundary voltages directly, with no likelihood to augment and no solver whose convergence depends on model fidelity. There is consequently no mechanism for numerical cancellation between generation and inversion. The concern does not vanish, however---a classifier trained on one discretisation could still learn features specific to it.

Two pieces of evidence bear on this. The mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}) quantifies discretisation error at 8--18\% for the spatially extended classes, and the cross-mesh transfer matrix shows a model trained on the medium mesh loses only 4.4\,pp when evaluated on meshes it never saw. More tellingly, the dominant learned behaviour is discounting a block-structured electrode offset, which is a property of the electrode model rather than of the interior discretisation. A classifier exploiting mesh-specific numerical structure would not be expected to remain stable across independently generated noise realisations \emph{and} across three discretisations, which it does.

\paragraph{Noise augmentation as implicit approximation-error compensation.} Viewed through Equation~\ref{eq:approx-error}, the electrode-level noise components perform a role analogous to Bayesian approximation-error compensation. The contact-impedance and electrode-bias terms model exactly the electrode--substrate interface idealisation that the Complete Electrode Model introduces, and the finding that electrode bias dominates the gap can be read as evidence that the dominant approximation error in this system is not mesh discretisation or dimensional reduction but the idealisation of the electrode interface---consistent with \textcite{vilhunen2002}. Where the Bayesian approach characterises $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ statistically and incorporates it into the likelihood, noise-augmented classification achieves a comparable effect by enriching the training distribution.

\section{Deployment Readiness}
\label{sec:disc-deployment}

\paragraph{Sensitivity to modelling assumptions.} The parameter sweep (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}) shows the conclusions are insensitive to measurement \gls{snr} across 60--20\,dB and to contact-impedance variation across 5--25\%, which matters because contact impedance is the least well-constrained parameter in the model. Accuracy does fall sharply at quadrupled electrode bias and at 8-bit \gls{adc} resolution, indicating that these two parameters bound the regime in which the findings hold. Read as engineering guidance these results would suggest prioritising electrode placement tolerance and \gls{adc} bit depth over front-end noise performance, but that reading should be treated cautiously as the sweep varies one simulated parameter at a time about a single operating point, and the electrode-bias threshold in particular depends on the assumed bias distribution being correct. The finding is offered as a robustness check on the modelling assumptions rather than as a specification.

\paragraph{Predicted confidence cannot be trusted.} Under domain shift the clean-trained model reports 99.7\% mean confidence while achieving 19.7\% accuracy. A prosthetic reporting near-certainty while performing at chance would supply actively misleading feedback to both user and grasp controller, and no downstream logic can recover a signal the softmax does not carry. That a distribution-matched model restores the correspondence is encouraging but does not license complacency, since the deployment distribution is never known exactly. Quantified uncertainty is integral to the Bayesian \gls{eit} tradition \parencite{kaipio2005statistical}; that ML-based classification has largely abandoned it for bare point predictions is a regression that should be reversed before any deployment claim is made.

\paragraph{Realistic expectations.} At approximately 76\% five-class accuracy under matched noise the system is not deployment-ready, and that figure is an upper bound for a simulation-only study rather than a projection for hardware. The per-class analysis is more informative than the aggregate. Detecting that contact has occurred is reliable, whereas characterising what kind is not, with point contact, combining the smallest spatial footprint with the least faithful simulation, weakest throughout. A staged deployment using contact detection for control while treating finer discrimination as advisory would be consistent with the evidence.

These conclusions complement rather than compete with hardware advances. \textcite{dong2025lattice} improve durability and sensitivity through silicone-encapsulated lattice hydrogels, though on short cycle counts and with Gaussian-only robustness analysis. Morphology optimisation improves the signal available at the electrodes; noise-aware training addresses the measurement uncertainty that remains. Since the present work locates the dominant gap at the electrode interface rather than in the substrate, the two address different bottlenecks and would be expected to compose.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The following limitations bound the generalisability of these findings.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No hardware validation.} All results are simulation-based, and the noise model is literature-justified rather than calibrated against a specific device. Since electrode bias is the component these results identify as decisive, an error in its distributional form would propagate directly to the central conclusions. This is the most important item of future work.

    \item \textbf{Independence of electrode bias.} The assumption that per-electrode offsets are independent and uniformly distributed suits rigidly mounted electrodes, but is questionable for the stretchable substrate this work targets. Under strain a hydrogel skin deforms as a sheet, so electrode displacements would be spatially \emph{correlated} rather than independent, leading to a difference in the structure of the distortion, not merely its magnitude. This yields a testable prediction. Correlated bias occupies a lower-dimensional subspace than independent bias, so a convolutional model should discount it more readily, and the 75.81\% reported here may be pessimistic in that specific respect. Measuring the true bias covariance on a physical sensor would settle this and is the natural first hardware experiment.

    \item \textbf{Incompleteness of the noise model.} Four error sources are modelled; several documented others are not, including boundary deformation, temporal drift and frequency-dependent interface impedance (Table~\ref{tab:error-sources}). As argued in Section~\ref{sec:method-noise-rationale}, this makes the reported gap a conservative lower bound (the collapse of clean-trained models is robust to it, since further sources could only worsen transfer), but it bounds the sufficiency claim of Section~\ref{sec:res-ablation-minimal} to the modelled family. Whether a two-component model remains sufficient once drift and geometric deformation are included is untested.

    \item \textbf{2D geometry.} The forward model uses a 2D cross-section, omitting out-of-plane current flow and inter-ring electrode coupling. As argued in Section~\ref{sec:disc-approx-error}, this is a deterministic discrepancy that noise augmentation cannot compensate.

    \item \textbf{Static single-frame classification.} Temporal correlations are not exploited, and temporal drift, which is documented over hours in hydrogel substrates, is explicitly outside the model's scope, since it cannot be represented within a single measurement frame.

\end{enumerate}

These limitations are inherent to a simulation-first design chosen to isolate the effect of noise augmentation under controlled conditions. They constrain the confidence with which specific accuracy figures can be projected onto hardware, but do not undermine the principal findings, which are comparative and structural. This finding is that the gap is dominated by a structured electrode-level error, and that modelling it is necessary and modelling two components is sufficient, and that the choice of training regime is a characterised trade-off rather than a single optimum.
