\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the findings in the context of the research objectives (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), prior literature (Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}), and deployment implications. It is structured around five themes: the nature of the sim-to-real gap (\S\ref{sec:disc-gap}), approximation error and the inverse crime (\S\ref{sec:disc-approx-error}), the augmentation paradox (\S\ref{sec:disc-paradox}), class-level vulnerability (\S\ref{sec:disc-class}), and deployment readiness (\S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}); a short synthesis (\S\ref{sec:disc-synthesis}) precedes the limitations.

\section{Nature of the Simulation-to-Reality Gap}
\label{sec:disc-gap}

The universal collapse to chance level under Clean$\to$Noisy evaluation ($\approx$20\% across all architectures and feature spaces) provides the clearest evidence yet that the sim-to-real gap in \gls{eit} tactile sensing is not addressable through model capacity alone. This finding extends the corruption robustness results of \textcite{hendrycks2019}---who demonstrated up to 40\% degradation for image classifiers---to a more extreme regime where multi-component noise completely eliminates learned representations. The contrast is statistically robust under paired testing ($p < 0.0001$) and corresponds to a complete distributional mismatch between clean and noisy feature spaces---a more extreme regime than the partial degradations typically studied in domain adaptation, where test-time data retains some overlap with the training distribution.

Critically, the noise-component decomposition localises the problem to a single dominant component: electrode positioning bias contributes 99.8\% of the total noise vector L2 magnitude (see derivation in Section~\ref{sec:method-noise}). This structural insight reframes the sim-to-real challenge: it is not fundamentally a random-noise problem but a \emph{systematic bias problem}. Each electrode acquires a fixed but unknown offset that is constant within its 13 measurements but varies between electrodes, producing block-structured corruption in the 208-D vector. This connects directly to the electrode modelling concerns raised by \textcite{vilhunen2002} and \textcite{kolehmainen1997}, who documented similar structured artefacts in reconstruction contexts but did not evaluate their impact on downstream classification tasks.

The CNN's superiority on noisy data (+18pp over the next-best model, RF at 57.9\%) can be attributed to its convolutional architecture. Kernels of size 5 spanning adjacent measurements naturally learn to detect and compensate for the block-structured bias pattern, functioning as implicit per-electrode bias-subtraction filters. This architectural advantage is absent in fully-connected models (MLP) and kernel methods (SVM) which treat each of the 208 measurements independently without exploiting their cyclic structure. The finding supports the argument of \textcite{lecun2015} that inductive biases matching the data structure are essential for robust generalisation, and extends this principle from spatial images to 1D measurement sequences with periodic structure. Notably \textcite{chen2023} also employed a CNN and reported flat high accuracy across models; under their Gaussian-only protocol the +18\,pp architectural gap observed here would be invisible because all architectures saturate, indicating that the multi-component noise regime is what makes architectural choice consequential rather than the choice itself.

The Gaussian-only comparison provides perhaps the most important contextualisation for the broader \gls{eit} community. Under the noise model assumed by \textcite{yao2022} and \textcite{chen2023}, \emph{all} models achieve $>$99\% accuracy regardless of training strategy. This means prior reported accuracies of 98--99\% under Gaussian noise \parencite{yao2022} do not demonstrate meaningful robustness, they merely confirm that additive Gaussian noise at typical SNR levels is trivially handled. Only the multi-component model exposes genuine vulnerability, motivating this work's contribution.

\section{Approximation Error and the Inverse Crime}
\label{sec:disc-approx-error}

An important methodological consideration for any simulation-trained classifier is the relationship between the \emph{inverse crime} \parencite{wirgin2004inverse, kaipio2007statistical} and the broader \emph{approximation error} framework \parencite{kaipio2005statistical}. In the classical inverse-problem literature, the inverse crime occurs when the same numerical discretisation is used to both generate synthetic data and solve the inverse problem, producing unrealistically favourable results because discretisation errors cancel exactly. \textcite{kaipio2007statistical} formalised this within a Bayesian framework, demonstrating that unaccounted modelling errors (mesh discretisation, dimensional reduction, material simplification) constitute a structured approximation error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ that must be statistically characterised to avoid systematic bias in the solution:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\text{measured}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}} \approx \mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}(\sigma) + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}
\label{eq:approx-error}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}$ denote the true and simplified forward operators respectively, and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}} = \mathcal{F}_{\text{accurate}}(\sigma) - \mathcal{F}_{\text{approximate}}(\sigma)$ captures the systematic discrepancy. \textcite{nissinen2009compensation} demonstrated experimentally that compensating for this approximation error---by augmenting the noise model with a statistical characterisation of $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$---substantially improves \gls{eit} reconstruction quality on real hardware.

\paragraph{Applicability to classification.} The standard approximation error framework is designed for \emph{inverse problems}, augmenting a Bayesian likelihood function with the statistics of the modelling discrepancy. The present work does not solve an inverse problem---it classifies voltage vectors directly, bypassing reconstruction entirely. There is no likelihood function to augment, and no iterative solver whose convergence depends on model fidelity. This fundamental distinction substantially reduces the severity of the inverse crime concern: the classifier operates on the \emph{output} of the forward model (boundary voltages) rather than using the forward model \emph{within} an inversion loop, meaning there is no opportunity for numerical cancellation between generation and solution discretisations.

Nevertheless, the \emph{principle} of approximation error remains relevant in a more subtle form. The forward model introduces systematic biases through several simplifications (Table~\ref{tab:approx-errors}), and a classifier trained exclusively on data from one model instance could learn to exploit artefacts of that specific discretisation rather than physically meaningful features.

\paragraph{Noise augmentation as implicit approximation error compensation.} A novel interpretation emerges when viewing the noise model through the lens of \textcite{kaipio2005statistical}: the four-component noise augmentation implicitly compensates for a subset of the forward model's approximation errors. Specifically, the contact impedance component ($\sigma_z = 10\%$, Equation~\ref{eq:noise-impedance}) models precisely the electrode--substrate interface discrepancy that the CEM idealises; the electrode bias component ($b_{\max} = 0.02$, Equation~\ref{eq:noise-bias}) captures the structured measurement offsets that arise from geometric simplification of electrode placement. These are exactly the categories of approximation error that \textcite{nissinen2009compensation} demonstrated require explicit compensation in reconstruction contexts. Where the Bayesian approach characterises $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\text{approx}}$ statistically and incorporates it into the likelihood, noise-augmented classification achieves an analogous effect through training-time distribution enrichment: exposing the classifier to the \emph{range} of possible approximation-error-induced perturbations, forcing it to learn representations invariant to these structured biases.

This connection has a significant implication. The observation that electrode bias accounts for 99.8\% of total noise L2 magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:disc-gap}) can be reinterpreted as evidence that the dominant approximation error is not mesh discretisation or dimensional reduction, but rather the idealisation of the \emph{electrode--substrate interface}---consistent with \textcite{vilhunen2002}'s finding that contact impedance modelling errors dominate reconstruction artefacts in real \gls{eit} systems. The noise model's electrode-level components therefore address the \emph{most critical} approximation error, even though they were originally motivated by measurement noise rather than forward-model discrepancy.

\paragraph{Mesh-level inverse crime.} The mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}, Table~\ref{tab:mesh-convergence}) shows that mesh-level approximation error is class-dependent. For the four spatially-extended classes (light touch, firm press, distributed contact) the mean relative voltage difference between the medium and fine discretisations is 8--18\,\%---of comparable order of magnitude to, but smaller than, the dominant electrode-bias component (which contributes 99.8\,\% of total noise L2 magnitude in the multi-component model). For the point-contact class the mesh-error figure (77\,\%) is not a meaningful convergence statistic because the contact radius is intrinsically sub-mesh-scale at all tractable refinements; the discretisation is therefore better characterised as a deliberate representational choice (single-element perturbation via the nearest-element fallback in \texttt{generate\_sample.m}) than as a numerical artefact. The stronger evidence against a mesh-level inverse crime is functional: the CNN's robustness to noise at 3$\times$ training severity (94.5\,\% accuracy) demonstrates that the learned features are insensitive to perturbations far exceeding any plausible discretisation artefact. Were the classifier exploiting mesh-specific numerical structure, it would degrade under noise that disrupts such structure---the opposite of what is observed. This constitutes a functional, classification-specific test of mesh independence that complements the traditional dual-mesh approach used in inverse-problem settings \parencite{kaipio2007statistical}.

\paragraph{Residual uncompensated errors.} The 2D$\to$3D geometric reduction remains the largest uncharacterised approximation error. Out-of-plane current flow in a real 3D sensor produces voltage patterns with no analogue in the 2D model---a systematic, structured discrepancy that cannot be compensated through random noise augmentation because it is deterministic and geometry-dependent. This represents a genuine residual inverse crime that future work must address through either 3D forward modelling or empirical calibration against physical hardware. Critically, this is a \emph{model fidelity} limitation rather than a \emph{noise model} limitation---the domain randomisation framework can only compensate for errors that manifest as stochastic perturbations to the measurement vector, not deterministic geometric biases.

\section{The Augmentation Paradox}
\label{sec:disc-paradox}

The most counter-intuitive finding is the complete failure of online noise augmentation (19.8\%) despite the success of fixed-noise training on the pre-generated noisy dataset (76.1\%). At first glance this contradicts the established domain randomisation literature \parencite{tobin2017} and the data-augmentation best practices surveyed by \textcite{shorten2019}, both of which predict that exposing a model to a wider range of corruptions during training should improve target-domain generalisation. The result is therefore worth dissecting carefully---particularly because the natural objection (that the 76.1\% itself reflects an inverse-crime-style coincidence between train- and test-time noise generation) does not in fact survive scrutiny.

\paragraph{The 76.1\% is not an artefact of generator equivalence.} A latent concern with any matched train/test noise pipeline is whether the classifier exploits sub-parametric structure of the specific generator implementation rather than the parametric noise distribution itself. To test this directly, the noisy-trained CNN was evaluated on the clean test split corrupted by an independent Python noise generator (\texttt{apply\_noise\_batch\_vectorised}) seeded with ten different random states (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}). The training data, by contrast, were generated by MATLAB's \texttt{add\_noise.m} from a single \texttt{rng(42)} stream. Despite the complete generator change, accuracy remained at $58.3\,\% \pm 0.7\,\%$ versus an in-pipeline reference of $57.7\,\%$---a $-0.6$\,pp drop. Were the headline result propped up by shared generator structure, this manipulation would degrade it substantially; it does not. The noise-matched model has therefore learned representations that are insensitive to the specific generator and depend only on the parametric noise distribution. This is a non-trivial sim-to-real claim: the model accommodates a corruption family rather than memorising its source.

\paragraph{Why online augmentation collapses.} The genuine mechanism behind the online-augmentation failure is more elementary than ``persistence'' arguments suggest, and follows from the random-per-epoch sampling rule alone. Consider a clean sample $\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}}$ that, across training, is presented as $\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}} + \mathbf{b}_t$ with $\mathbf{b}_t \sim p(\mathbf{b})$ drawn afresh at each epoch $t$. The only across-epoch invariant is $\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}}$ itself; the loss landscape rewards a representation that suppresses $\mathbf{b}_t$ rather than one that uses it. Under the zero-mean structure of the noise model ($\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{b}] = \mathbf{0}$ for the additive components), the time-averaged input over many epochs satisfies $\mathbb{E}_t[\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}} + \mathbf{b}_t] = \mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}}$, so a smooth classifier will converge to $f(\mathbf{x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{clean}})$---a clean-signal extractor that achieves 98.5\% on clean data but cannot accommodate the persistent bias encountered at any individual test frame. The training curves and confusion-matrix patterns reported in Section~\ref{sec:res-main} are consistent with this account: the augmented and clean-trained CNNs converge to near-identical clean-domain features and exhibit the same Clean$\to$Noisy failure mode.

\paragraph{Refinement to the Tobin et al.\ argument.} Domain randomisation \parencite{tobin2017} is typically motivated by the assumption that the deployment environment is itself a draw from the randomisation distribution: each test frame sees a fresh sample of the simulated nuisance variable. \gls{eit} hardware violates that assumption. Each physical electrode has an effectively fixed per-device offset that is constant across the measurement frames a single device produces---block-structured corruption that is random across devices but deterministic within one. Random-per-epoch augmentation therefore presents the classifier with a statistical regime (i.i.d.\ per frame) that the test distribution (correlated within frame, fixed across the device's lifetime) does not match. The pre-generated noisy dataset succeeds by precisely the opposite property: it preserves the within-frame correlation structure, allowing the CNN's convolutional kernels to learn implicit per-electrode bias-subtraction filters (Section~\ref{sec:disc-gap}). The lesson is not that domain randomisation fails in general but that its applicability is conditional on a match between the randomisation distribution and the within-frame correlation structure of the deployment noise---a refinement directly relevant to any transducer modality with persistent per-device characteristics, including strain gauges and capacitive arrays.

\paragraph{An open methodological caveat.} A complementary fixed-bias augmentation experiment (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}) attempted to interpolate between the random-per-epoch and pre-generated-dataset regimes by sampling noise once per training sample and holding it fixed across epochs. As reported, this configuration failed at 19.9\,\%. On closer inspection of the implementation (\texttt{src/eit\_sim2real/experiments/additional.py::FixedBiasDataset}), the experiment is confounded: training noise is applied in the post-scaler feature space at parameter magnitudes calibrated for raw voltages, whereas test noise is applied to raw voltages and subsequently scaled with the same robust scaler. The validation loss profile (best $\approx 23.0$ versus $<1.0$ for the pre-generated noisy CNN) is consistent with this normalisation mismatch rather than with a genuine learning failure. The fixed-bias result is therefore inconclusive as evidence about persistence-versus-randomness, and the mechanism account above rests on the online-augmentation expectation argument and the cross-generator different-draw test rather than on this experiment. A corrected fixed-bias run is identified as a small but worthwhile follow-up in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.

\section{Per-Class Vulnerability and Physical Interpretation}
\label{sec:disc-class}

The per-class robustness profiling reveals a physically interpretable vulnerability hierarchy. Light touch ($-$14.7pp at 3$\times$ severity) is most affected because its signal magnitude (L2 = 0.004) is only $\sim$2.4$\times$ the residual noise floor (L2 = 0.005 after bias removal). As noise severity increases, the signal-to-noise ratio approaches unity and the class boundary erodes. This connects to the detection threshold analysis of \textcite{boone1996}, who established a minimum conductivity contrast of $\sim$2\% for reliable EIT detection---light touch operates near this boundary by design (5--15\% $\Delta\sigma$). The vulnerability is also consistent with \gls{eit}'s well-documented non-uniform sensitivity field \parencite{adler2006}: interior regions far from the boundary electrodes contribute weaker boundary-voltage perturbations per unit conductivity change, so spatially-distributed light-touch contacts produce a measurement signature that is small in magnitude and biased toward the least-sensitive part of the sensitivity map, compounding the SNR-floor effect.

The t-SNE visualisations confirm that light touch and point contact clusters overlap under noise, as do firm press and distributed contact. This pairing is physically motivated: classes with similar L2 norms but different spatial extent become indistinguishable when spatial information is corrupted. The model conflates \emph{magnitude} with \emph{extent}---a physically reasonable confusion given that both produce similar integral voltage perturbations, differing primarily in the spatial distribution of those perturbations across electrode pairs.

The Noisy$\to$Clean asymmetry (52.6\%) reveals a complementary failure mode: the noisy-trained CNN never encounters the zero-vector class 0 (no contact) without additive bias, and systematically misclassifies clean zero-vectors as light touch. This is not a model deficiency but a training distribution problem---addressable through mixed training with correct bias persistence, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-paradox}.

\section{Deployment Readiness and Safety Implications}
\label{sec:disc-deployment}

Three findings have direct implications for prosthetic deployment:

\paragraph{Calibration failure.} The catastrophic overconfidence (ECE = 0.795) under domain shift means that raw softmax outputs cannot serve as uncertainty indicators in safety-critical applications \parencite{guo2017calibration}: a prosthetic skin reporting 99.6\% confidence while achieving 20\% accuracy would provide dangerously misleading feedback to both the user and any autonomous grasp controller. Post-hoc calibration methods (temperature scaling) or explicit uncertainty quantification (Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles) are therefore prerequisite for deployment---an aspect entirely absent from prior \gls{eit} classification studies \parencite{yao2022, chen2023}. The ECE improvement from 0.795 to 0.053 when training and test distributions match further suggests that calibration failure here is a \emph{domain shift phenomenon} rather than an architectural deficiency, and that calibration metrics should be reported alongside accuracy in any future \gls{eit} classification benchmark.

\paragraph{Noise model completeness.} The sensitivity analysis confirms that results generalise across physically plausible parameter ranges (accuracy $>$97.5\% for all parameters except extreme 20\,dB SNR). This suggests the noise model captures the dominant degradation modes robustly. However, temporal drift remains unaddressed. \textcite{lee2019} documented progressive baseline shifts in hydrogel substrates over 24-hour periods, a sequential phenomenon requiring temporal models rather than per-frame augmentation.

\paragraph{Component prioritisation for hardware design.} The ablation identifies contact impedance as the fastest-degrading component under severity scaling ($-$4.5pp from 0$\times$ to 3$\times$), suggesting that electrode-substrate interface quality is the highest-priority hardware improvement. Quantisation (16-bit) is effectively invisible, confirming that standard ADC resolution is more than sufficient for \gls{eit} tactile sensing. This empirical prioritisation---electrode interface $>$ noise floor $>$ measurement chain $>$ digitisation---provides actionable guidance for hardware designers, absent from prior computational studies. The finding that contact impedance degrades performance multiplicatively (consistent with its effect on drive current magnitude) while Gaussian noise is merely additive further clarifies why the multi-component model is essential: the dominant degradation mechanism is non-additive and therefore cannot be characterised by SNR alone.

\paragraph{Economic and accessibility implications.} The component-level priority ranking has direct cost implications for accessible deployment. \gls{eit}'s clinical appeal rests on delivering large-area sensing from few electrodes at materially lower cost than dense arrays \parencite{who2017prosthetics, mcdonald2021}. The findings here operationalise that argument: 16-bit quantisation is unnecessary, so premium ADCs can be substituted with cheaper alternatives; the dominant degradation source is the electrode--substrate interface, directing scarce engineering effort toward substrate quality rather than instrumentation precision; and simulation-based prioritisation of this kind runs on commodity hardware, avoiding expensive iterative hardware prototyping. These are preconditions for the cost-down path that prosthetic deployment in low- and middle-income settings requires, where most of the unmet need lies.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:disc-synthesis}

The five themes above converge on a single account. Electrode positioning bias is simultaneously the dominant noise component (99.8\% of energy, \S\ref{sec:disc-gap}), the dominant approximation error (the electrode-interface idealisation in the \gls{cem}, \S\ref{sec:disc-approx-error}), the principal beneficiary of within-frame correlation preservation (the mechanism by which the noisy-trained CNN reaches 76.1\%, \S\ref{sec:disc-paradox}), the strongest predictor of per-class vulnerability (light touch is most exposed because its signal magnitude is closest to the residual bias floor, \S\ref{sec:disc-class}), and the highest-leverage hardware investment for deployment (\S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}). Bridging the simulation-to-reality gap in classification-mode \gls{eit} is therefore not a generic robustness problem but a specific interface-modelling problem; this is the central methodological lesson of the dissertation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Several limitations bound the generalisability of these findings and must be acknowledged transparently:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Matched parametric noise distribution.} The 76.1\% Noisy$\to$Noisy result uses the same parametric noise family for train and test data. A natural concern is whether the CNN has learned genuine noise-invariant features or has merely exploited generator-specific structure---an inverse-crime-style coincidence in which sub-parametric regularities of the simulation pipeline (RNG implementation, ordering of additive and multiplicative steps, floating-point quantisation) leak across train and test.
    
    The strongest available evidence against this concern is the cross-generator different-draw test (Section~\ref{sec:res-memorisation}). The model is trained on data produced by MATLAB's \texttt{add\_noise.m} from a single \texttt{rng(42)} stream and then evaluated on the clean test split re-corrupted by an independent Python implementation (\texttt{apply\_noise\_batch\_vectorised}) under ten different random seeds. The two pipelines share only their parametric noise distribution; they differ in RNG family, in the order in which the four noise components are composed, and in the numerical implementation of quantisation and clipping. Performance is unchanged ($58.3\,\% \pm 0.7\,\%$ versus a $57.7\,\%$ in-pipeline reference, a $-0.6$\,pp shift), directly falsifying the hypothesis that this noise-augmented model exploits generator equivalence between train and test. The use of five independent stratified holdouts in the headline experiment (std $= 0.6\,\%$) and the graceful degradation observed at $3\times$ training severity (94.5\,\%, beyond the training-time noise envelope) provide complementary evidence against pure noise memorisation.
    
    What this evidence \emph{does not} establish is transfer to a real \gls{eit} system, where the parametric noise distribution itself may differ (multimodal or heavy-tailed bias, frequency-dependent contact impedance, temporal drift) from the literature-derived parameters used here. Cross-distribution evaluation---training under one parametric family and testing under a measurably different one---and ultimately hardware validation are therefore identified as essential future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}. The 76.1\% should be read as an upper bound on what noise-augmented training can recover when the parametric noise model is correct, not as a projected hardware-deployment accuracy.
    
    \item \textbf{2D geometry.} The forward model uses a 2D cross-section; 3D geometries introduce additional sensitivity patterns, out-of-plane current flow, and electrode coupling effects not captured here \parencite{adler2006}. Extension to 3D would increase the feature space substantially ($>$1000 measurements) and may alter the relative impact of noise components.
    
    \item \textbf{Sub-mesh-scale point contact.} The mesh convergence study (Section~\ref{sec:method-mesh}, Table~\ref{tab:mesh-convergence}) established that the point-contact class (radius 0.02--0.05 on a unit-circle mesh) is intrinsically a sub-mesh-scale perturbation: even at the fine refinement, only 2.5 elements on average intersect the contact region, and 4 of 20 samples are under-resolved ($\le 1$ element intersected). The data-generation pipeline therefore represents point contact as a single-element conductivity perturbation via a nearest-element fallback, in the spirit of a discrete localised load \parencite{kim2019}, rather than a fully resolved disk. Real point contacts on hardware would produce localised pressure spikes that no FEM at this geometry could fully resolve; bridging this gap requires either a 3D forward model with substantially refined local meshing near the contact, or empirical calibration against physical hardware. This is identified as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}. The implication for the present findings is that the point-contact class should be interpreted as a \emph{representative} of high-pressure focal contacts rather than a quantitatively faithful simulation of one; the noise-robustness conclusions for this class therefore characterise the classifier's behaviour on a stylised single-element perturbation, and quantitative transfer to real point-contact stimuli will require model-fidelity improvements orthogonal to the noise augmentation framework developed here.
    
    \item \textbf{Static single-frame classification.} Temporal correlations in sequential measurements, which could aid classification through pattern-over-time features, are not exploited \parencite{silvera-tawil2012eit}. Recurrent or attention-based architectures processing measurement sequences may achieve superior noise robustness by leveraging temporal redundancy.
    
    \item \textbf{No hardware validation.} All results are simulation-based; the noise model is literature-justified but not empirically calibrated against a specific physical system. The assumed Gaussian distribution for electrode bias is a modelling choice---real bias distributions may be multimodal or heavy-tailed.
    
    \item \textbf{Single-seed ablation protocol.} The ablation study uses a single random seed with extended training (200 epochs) for computational tractability. While the five-seed main experiment provides robust estimates, the ablation results should be interpreted as indicative rather than statistically confirmed.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations are inherent to the simulation-first research design chosen to isolate the noise augmentation effect under controlled conditions. They do not invalidate the core findings---which demonstrate the \emph{principle} of noise-matched training and identify electrode bias as the dominant gap component---but constrain the quantitative confidence with which specific accuracy figures can be projected onto real hardware deployment.
