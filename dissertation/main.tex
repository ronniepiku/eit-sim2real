\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) offers a promising sensing modality for electronic prosthetic skin, enabling wide-area tactile perception from a few boundary electrodes. However, classifiers trained on idealised simulation data fail when deployed on real hardware. This dissertation investigates whether a physically motivated multi-component noise model can bridge this gap, and which of these components such a model must contain.

A synthetic data set of 25,000 EIT voltage measurements across five touch classes was generated using the EIDORS forward modelling framework, simulating a 16-electrode ionic hydrogel prosthetic skin. Four classifiers were evaluated across a four-condition train/test matrix, with significance established by stratified 5-fold cross-validation and every noise component ablated under both matched and deployment-realistic evaluation.

All models collapse to chance ($\approx$20\%) under domain mismatch, irrespective of architecture or feature representation, identifying the gap as distributional rather than a limitation of model capacity. Noise-matched training recovers substantial performance, the 1D-CNN reaching 75.8\% $\pm$ 0.6 (+55.8 percentage points). Direct energy decomposition shows electrode positioning bias that effectively accounts for all noise energy, exceeding the light-touch signal by roughly a factor of 41; because that bias is block-structured rather than random, the task remains tractable, and convolutional architectures gain a 17.9-point advantage over baselines that treat changeably.

The ablation yields the central result, and depends on how it is evaluated. Judged on matched conditions, Gaussian noise appears benign and electrode bias most damaging; judged against the full corruption a deployed device actually encounters, this reverses entirely---noise models omitting electrode bias perform at chance, while Gaussian noise plus electrode bias alone reaches 69.3\%, statistically level with the full four-component model. Contact impedance and quantisation contribute nothing measurable. Severity scaling reveals a trade-off rather than a winner: fixed-noise training peaks at its training severity but loses 15 points by 3$\times$, whereas randomised training is far flatter and overtakes it at approximately 2.2$\times$. Calibration analysis shows catastrophic overconfidence under domain shift (ECE~=~0.800 at 19.7\% accuracy) that is recovered under matched training (ECE~=~0.047), establishing miscalibration here as a symptom of domain shift rather than of architecture.

These findings identify the minimal noise model required for deployable EIT tactile classification, provide hardware tolerances expressed in quantities engineers specify directly, and demonstrate that the evaluation protocol used in component ablation can invert the design guidance derived from it.
